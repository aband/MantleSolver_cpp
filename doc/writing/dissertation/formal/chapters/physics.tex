\section{Mechanics of Mantle Dynamics}
Fluid dynamics of the mixture of two phase flow has been extensively discussed.
We will disscuss the three governing equations of mass and momentum referring
to the similar discussion in \cite{AveragedEquationsTwoPhase}, 
\cite{MagmaPhysics} and \cite{DynamicsPartiallyMoltenRock}.
Considering a conservation equation for given volumetric variable $\gamma$ in 
a representative volume element $V$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generalconservationlaw}
		  \frac{d}{dt} \int_{V} \gamma d\mathbf{v} = 
		  - \int_{\partial V} \mathbf{f} \cdot d\sigma + \int_{V} S d\mathbf{v},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{f}$ represents flux travelling across the boundary of a representative 
volume element $V$. The minus sign indicates that the positive direction aligns with
normal unit vector pointing outwards. $S$, source/sink term, represents the volumetric
production rate of $\gamma$.
We can apply Gauss's theorem to obtain differential form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conservationlawpde}
\frac{\partial \gamma}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{f} = S.
\end{equation}
We also define the quantity of phase fraction rigorously with the help of an 
indicator function admitting that multiple phase cannot coexist at the same
spatial point, which are regarded as immiscible phases.
We define indicator function,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:indicatorfunction}
		  i(\mathbf{x}) = \left \{ 
		  \begin{aligned}
					 &1 \quad \text{phase}_i,\\
					 &0 \quad \text{not phase}_i.
		  \end{aligned}
		  \right.
\end{equation}
We then define macroscopic phase fraction function with the help of \eqref{eq:indicatorfunction},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:porosityfunction}
		  \phi_i = \frac{1}{\delta \mathbf{v}} 
		  \int_{\delta \mathbf{v}} i(\mathbf{x}) d\mathbf{v}.
\end{equation}
In the beginning of my discussion, we will discuss two immiscible phases liquid magma 
and solid mantle. Phase fraction of liquid magam is labelled as $\phi_l$ and phase
fraction of solid mantle is labelled as $\phi_s$, where $\phi_l + \phi_s = 1$ is
always attained. Phase fraction can also be regarded as volume fraction in macroscopic sense.
Substituting bulk density $\overline{\rho} = \phi_l \rho_l + \phi_s \rho_s$ for generalized 
volumetric variable $\gamma$ in equation \eqref{eq:generalconservationlaw}, we can obtain
conservation law for bulk mass,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:massconservation}
\frac{\partial \overline{\rho}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\rho \mathbf{v}} = 0,
\end{equation}
with the help of Boussinesq approximation ignoring compressibility of each individual
phase, and extend Boussinesq approximation further applying to density difference 
between each phase except body force term. We can write continuous function for two 
phase mixture simply as,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuousfunction}
\nabla \cdot \overline{\mathbf{v}} = 0.
\end{equation}
Substituing phase averaged momentum $\overline{\rho \mathbf{v}}$,
we then write conservation of momentum with phase averaged term,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:momentumconservation}
\frac{\partial \overline{\rho \mathbf{v}}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\rho \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{v}}  = 
\overline{\rho}\mathbf{g} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\mathbf{\tau}} - \nabla \overline{p},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{\tau}$ represents deviatoric stress.
Ignoring change of inertia term due to near zero Reynolds number in magma and mantle, 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:staticmomentumconservation}
\overline{\rho}\mathbf{g} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\mathbf{\tau}} - \nabla \overline{p} = 0,
\end{equation}
which represents the bulk conservation of momentum. 
This buld equation cannot determine two different velocities at the same time, hence, we need additional relationship
governing the behavior of liquid through a porous media.
We now take a closer look at the interphase force between solid and liquid phases.
We will provide a brief overview of how the individual equations governing the liquid and solid phases are derived, 
while more detailed and rigorous proofs can be found in the mixture theory literature like \cite{mixturetheory}.
We can write momentum balances for each individual phase as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phasemomentum}
\begin{aligned}
0 &= \phi_l \rho_l \mathbf{g} + \nabla \cdot (\phi_l \tau_l) - \nabla (\phi_l p_l) + \mathbf{I}_l,\\
0 &=  \phi_s \rho_s \mathbf{g} + \nabla \cdot (\phi_s \tau_s) - \nabla (\phi_s p_s) - \mathbf{I}_s,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{I}_l + \mathbf{I}_s = 0$ is the interphase force between solid and liquid phases.
We ignore deviatoric stress in liquid and keep isotropic stress only. 
We balance the remaining stress with viscous drag force and equilibrium term.
We can reach the following equation aligning with Darcy's Law:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:darcylaw}
		  \phi_l (\mathbf{v}_l - \mathbf{v}_s)
		  + \frac{k_{\phi_l}}{\mu_l}(\nabla p_l - \rho_f\mathbf{g})
	    = 0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Compaction}
We have an additional yet unique relationship regulating melt migration in mantle
dynamics. We now revisit the continuous equation~\eqref{eq:continuousfunction}, 
with the two phases considered separately taking melting into consideration
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuousequationPhase}
		  \begin{aligned}
					 \frac{\partial \phi_l }{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\phi_l 
					 \mathbf{v}_l) &= \Gamma, \\
					 \frac{\partial (1-\phi_l) }{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot ((1-\phi_l)
					 \mathbf{v}_s) &= -\Gamma,
		  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma$ is the unknown melting rate. We now focus on the solid phase 
equation to get the evolving compaction relationship as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solidmelting}
		  \frac{\partial \phi_l}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v}_s \cdot \nabla \phi_l = 
		  \Gamma + (1-\phi_l) \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s,
\end{equation}
where the divergence term $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s$ is often referred to as the 
compaction rate.
We can now approximate the static compaction relation as~\cite{MagmaDynamicsEnthalpyMethod}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compactionviscosity}
		  \xi_{\phi}\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s  =p_l - p_s, 
\end{equation}
where $\xi_{\phi}$ is often referred to as bulk viscosity or compaction viscosity. 
The form of compaction viscosity $\xi_{\phi}$
has been discussed extensively. \cite{MagmaPhysics} first introduced the concept of
compaction viscosity and relating the term with stress balance.
\cite{TwoPhaseModelCompaction} included surface tension into the 
force balance along with stress term. \cite{Compactioninamantlewithaverysmallmelt} discussed compaction
with the porosity being infinitesimal small. We approximate $\xi_\phi$ with a simple relationship as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bulkviscosity}
\xi_\phi = \frac{\mu_s}{\phi},
\end{equation}
which highlighting the fact that increased melting facilitates the compaction of the solid phase, while in the absence of melting, 
compaction is entirely eliminated.

\subsection{Full System}
Combining equations~\eqref{eq:continuousfunction},~\eqref{eq:staticmomentumconservation},~\eqref{eq:darcylaw} 
and~\eqref{eq:compactionviscosity},
We finally arive with the full flow mechanical system,
\begin{alignat}{2}
    \label{eq:darcy}
		  &\phi_l (\mathbf{v}_l - \mathbf{v}_s)
		  + \frac{k_{\phi_l}}{\mu_l}(\nabla p_l - \rho_f\mathbf{g})
	    &&= 0,\\
	\label{eq:continuous}
		  &\nabla \cdot (\phi_l\mathbf{v}_l + \phi_s\mathbf{v}_s) &&=0,\\
	\label{eq:stokes}
		  &\nabla\bar p - \nabla \cdot \tau(\mathbf{v}_s) + \overline{\rho}\mathbf{g}&&= 0,\\
	\label{eq:compaction}
		  &\mu_s\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s - \phi_l(p_l - p_s) &&=0, 
\end{alignat}
where the deviatoric stress of the solid is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deviatoricstress}
		  \tau(\mathbf{v}_s)
		  = 2\mu_s\phi_s\big(\mathcal{D} \mathbf{v}_s - \tfrac{1}{3}\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s \mathbf{I}\big),
\end{equation}
and $\mathcal{D} \mathbf{v}_s=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{v}_s + \nabla\mathbf{v}_s^T)$ is the symmetric gradient.
Moreover, the relative permeability is $k_{\phi_l} = k_0 \phi_l^{2+2\Theta}$, where $\Theta \in [0,1/2]$. $\mu_s$ 
and $\mu_l$ are dynamic viscosities for solid and liquid correspondingly.

\subsection{Scaled Formulation}
The Darcy part will mathematically degenerate in regions where the porosity is zero.
To handle this issue, we use the formulation developed 
previously~\cite{DegenerateDarcy}. We first define pressure potentials as,
\begin{equation}
	q_l = p_l - \rho_l g z\quad\text{and}\quad q_s = p_s - \rho_l g z,
\end{equation}
where $g=|\mathbf{g}|$ and $z$ is the depth, so $\mathbf{g}=g\nabla z$. Note that $\rho_l$ is used to define both potentials, so that with $q=\bar q = \bar p - \rho_l g z$,
\begin{equation*}
	p_l - p_s = q_l - q_s = \frac{1}{1-\phi_l}(q_l - q).
\end{equation*}
We can then rewrite the system \eqref{eq:darcy}--\eqref{eq:compaction} omitting the subscript of liquid volumetric fraction,
\begin{alignat}{2}
		  &\phi\mathbf{v}_r + \frac{k_0\phi^{2+2\Theta}}{\mu_l}\nabla q_l &&= 0,\\
		  &\mu_s\nabla \cdot (\phi\mathbf{v}_r) + \frac{\phi}{1-\phi}(q_l-q) &&=0,\\
  		  &\nabla q - \nabla \cdot \tau(\mathbf{v}_s) &&= -(1-\phi)\rho_r\mathbf{g},\\
		  &\mu_s\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s - \frac{\phi}{1-\phi}(q_l-q) &&=0.
\end{alignat}
where relative velocity $\mathbf{v}_r = \mathbf{v}_l - \mathbf{v}_s$ and density difference 
$\rho_r = \rho_s - \rho_l$. We refer to $\phi$ as porosity in the later text.
Scaled variables are defined,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scaledVariables}
		  \tilde{\mathbf{v}}_r = \phi^{-\Theta}\mathbf{v}_r
	\quad\text{and}\quad
	\tilde{q}_l = \phi^{1/2}q_l,
\end{equation}
and the problem is reformulated as
\begin{alignat}{2}
\label{eq:darcy_flow_sc}
		  &\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_r
		  + \frac{k_0\phi^{1+\Theta}}{\mu_f}\nabla(\phi^{-1/2}\tilde{q}_l)
        &&= 0,\\
\label{eq:consMass_flow_sc}
		  &\mu_s\phi^{-1/2}\nabla \cdot(\phi^{1+\Theta}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_r)
        + \frac{1}{1-\phi}(\tilde{q}_l - \phi^{1/2}q) &&=0,\\
\label{eq:stokes_flow_sc}
		  &\nabla q - \nabla \cdot \tau(\mathbf{v}_s) &&= -(1-\phi)\rho_r \mathbf{g},\\
\label{eq:compaction_flow_sc}
		  &\mu_s\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s
        - \frac{\phi^{1/2}}{1-\phi}(\tilde{q}_l - \phi^{1/2}q) &&=0,
\end{alignat}
where the new scaled relative velocity maintain numerical stability in zero prosority region.

\cite{ConsequencesMeltTransport}, \cite{PartialMeltinginUpwellingMantleColumn}, \cite{MagmaDynamicsEnthalpyMethod} 
and \cite{DownwardOxidantTransport}. 
Both two species can be solid or liquid. Two species are immiscible as solid but miscible as liquid.
We consider a homogenous density $\rho_s$ for solid phase and $\rho_l$ for liquid phase.
With extened Boussinesq approximation later assuming no density difference except in body force term, 
we can make further assumption that $\rho_l = \rho_s = \rho$

Within the representative volume element $V$, $\gamma_{i,j}$ represents one quantity of the specie $i$ presenting in
phase $j$, where $i \in {1,2}$ and $j \in \{s,l\}$.   
The mass fraction $c_{i,j}$ of a given specie $i$ in phase $j$ is often regarded as concentration.
Hence, we can write transport equations for a individual specie $i$ assuming a pseudo melting term $\Gamma$,
\begin{alignat}{1}
\label{eq:conservationliquidindividual}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big( \phi_l \rho_l c_{i,l} \big)  + \nabla \cdot \big(\phi_l \rho_l c_{i,l} \mathbf{v}_l - \phi_l \rho_l \mathcal{D}_{i,l} \nabla c_{i,l} \big) &= \Gamma_i ,\\
\label{eq:conservationsolidindividual}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big( \phi_s \rho_s c_{i,s} \big)  + \nabla \cdot \big(\phi_s \rho_l c_{i,s} \mathbf{v}_s - \phi_s \rho_s \mathcal{D}_{i,s} \nabla c_{i,s} \big) &= -\Gamma_i ,
\end{alignat}
where we can safely get rid of dissusion term in solid phase.
Summing up equation\ref{eq:conservationliquidindividual} and equation\ref{eq:conservationsolidindividual}, 
we can write a advection-diffusion equation in phase averaged volumetric bulk variable,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:specietransport}
		  \frac{\partial \overline{\rho c_{i}}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \big( \overline{\rho c_{i} \mathbf{v}} - \phi_l \rho_l \mathcal{D}_{i,l} \nabla c_{i,l} \big) = 0,
\end{equation}
where the phase averaged volumetric bulk variable are defined as $\overline{\rho c_i} = \sum_{j\in \{s,l\}} \phi_j \rho_j c_{i,j}$ 
and $\overline{\rho c_i \mathbf{v}} = \sum_{j\in \{s,l\}} \phi_j \rho_j c_{i,j} \mathbf{v}_j$.
The subscript $i$ will be omitted since only one chemical specie is traced in this two-component system.
Extending Boussinessq approximation $\rho_l = \rho_s = \overline{\rho}$, the specie transport equation can be
simplified as,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:specietransportsimplified}
\frac{\partial \overline{c}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \big( \overline{ c \mathbf{v}} - \phi_l \mathcal{D}_{l} \nabla c_l \big) = 0.
\end{equation}

We write energy balance equation in the phase-averaged internal energy form first,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energybalanceinternalenergy}
\frac{\partial \overline{\rho u}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\rho u \mathbf{v}} - \nabla \cdot \overline{K} \nabla T = 
\overline{\rho \mathcal{Q}} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\sigma \cdot \mathbf{v}} + \overline{\rho \mathbf{v}} \cdot \mathbf{g}.
\end{equation}
On the left hand side, we have time dependent rate of internal energy chage, advection term and diffusion term.
On the right hand side, we have heating/cooling source $\mathcal{Q}$, work exerted by stress and work againt gravity $g$.
In a thermochemical equilibrium scenerio, we are replacing internal energy with enthalpy.
We can write energy balancing equation neglecting additional heating/cooling term $\mathcal{Q}$ and dissipative heating 
following the discussion in \cite{MagmaPhysics},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energyconservationenthalpy}
\frac{\partial \overline{\rho h}}{\partial t} + \rho c_p \nabla \cdot \overline{\mathbf{v}} T = 
\rho L \nabla \cdot \phi_s \mathbf{v}_s + \overline{K} \nabla^2 T + \rho \alpha_\rho T \overline{\mathbf{v}} \cdot \mathbf{g},
\end{equation}
where specific heat capacity $c_p$ is used for both 
components in solid and liquid phase.
Similar but slighted altered form was also discussed in \cite{MagmaDynamicsEnthalpyMethod}, where adiabatic cooling effect 
was added in terms of potential temperature\cite{ExtensionofLithosphere}.
We now divide $\rho c_p$ on both sides of the equation considering 
Boussinessq approximation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalizedenthalpy}
		  \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\mathbf{v}} T = 
		  \frac{L}{c_p} \nabla \cdot \phi_s \mathbf{v}_s + \kappa \nabla^2 T + 
		  \frac{\alpha_\rho}{c_p} T \overline{\mathbf{v}} \cdot \mathbf{g},
\end{equation}
where $H = \overline{h}/c_p$ is the bulk specific enthalpy and $\kappa = 
\overline{K}/\rho c_p$ is the heat diffusivity.

\subsection{Effective Velocity}
Effective velocity in equilibrium transport for concentration within liquid phase is discussed in \cite{GeochemicalConsequence}.
We also introduce our effective velocity, but instead of transporting concentration in liquid(magma), we transport bulk concentration.
Define partition coefficient $D_i = c_{i,s}/c_{i,f}$ and 
\begin{alignat}{1}
		  \label{eq:effectiveliquidcon}
		  (\phi_l + \phi_s D_i) c_{i,l} &= \overline{c}_i, \\
		  \label{eq:effectivesolidcon}
		  (\phi_l/D_i + \phi_s) c_{i,s} &= \overline{c}_i. 
\end{alignat}
Substitute $c_{i,l}$ and $c_{i,s}$ with equation \eqref{eq:effectiveliquidcon} and equation \eqref{eq:effectivesolidcon} into
bulk velocity $\overline{c_{i}\bold{v}} = \phi_s \bold{v}_s c_{i,s} + \phi_l \bold{v}_l c_{i,l}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:effectivevel}
		  \overline{c_{i}\bold{v}} = \phi_s \bold{v}_s c_{i,s} + \phi_l \bold{v}_l c_{i,l} = 
		  \bigg( \frac{\phi_l \bold{v}_l + D\phi_s \bold{v}_s}{\phi_l + \phi_s D} \bigg) \overline{c}_i.
\end{equation}
Hence, we get the proper expression for the effective velocity as 
$\bold{v}_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\phi_l \bold{v}_l + D_i\phi_s \bold{v}_s}{\phi_l + \phi_s D_i}$, 
where the partition coefficient $D$ can be obtained through phase behavior package.
If there is no melting $\phi_l = 0$, we can reterive $\mathbf{v}_{\text{eff}} = \mathbf{v}_s$.
If there is all melting $\phi_l = 1$, we can obtain $\mathbf{v}_{\text{eff}} = \mathbf{v}_l$.
Hence, the definition of effective velocity is well defined in those two boundary scenerios. We rewrite the transport of specie with effective velocity,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:effectivetransportspecie}
		  \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = 
		  \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{v}_{\text{eff}} C - \phi_l \mathcal{D}_l \nabla c_l) ,
		  = 0
\end{equation}
where $C = \overline{c}$ represents volumetric phase averaged 
(bulk) concentration of species.

\section{Eutectic Phase Behavior}
The enthalpy method requires an explicit relationship between temperature and enthalpy.
Embracing the fact that mid-ocean ridge forms at the place where temperature 
is generally lower, eutectic melting, where melting temperature is signaficantly
lowered with the presence of the secondary specie, is a suitable model for modelling
melting behavior at certain location.
The binary eutectic phase behavior describes the chemical behavior of 
two immiscible crystals from a completely miscible solution.
On a typical eutectic phase behavior diagram, we can locate any phase situation
using transported enthalpy and concentration. 
Eutectic melting in mantle was being described in work by~\cite{GenerationCompositionPartialMelts}.
Binary eutectic melting has also
been applied to water-salt-brine system in glacier~\cite{DownwardOxidantTransport}. 

\subsection{Eutectic Phase Parameters}
We approximate the melting temperature and eutectic temperature
at a given pressure using Calusius-Clapeyron relationship,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calusiusclapeyron}
\begin{aligned}
T_m &= T_{m0} + \nu^{-1} P, \\
T_e &= T_{e0} + \nu^{-1} P, \\
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\nu$ is the Clausius-Clapeyron constant.

In a eutectic system, we use bulk mass fraction and bulk enthalpy to locate
the current state of a given system.
$\mathcal{X}_i$ represents bulk mass fraction of specie $i$ 
and $\mathcal{X}_{i,e}$ represents eutectic mass fraction of specie $i$ in liquid.
The subscript $i$ will be omitted in the later context since only one specie
is being traced in transport equation.
Some assumptions are made for partially molten mantle system.
\begin{itemize}
\item Assuming thermodynamic equilibrium is achieved at every time step that phase state can be calculated
		  with phase diagram.
\item The composition of the second specie in the liquid formed by eutectic melting $\mathcal{X}_e$ is constant.  
\item Melting temperature $T_m$ and eutectic temperature $T_e$ are functions to pressure $T_m(P)$ and $T_e(P)$. 
\item Boussinessq approximation is extended to phase behavior as well $\rho_l = \rho_s$.
\item Assuming no density difference between two solid components.
\item Assuming constant thermal expansion and specific heat capacity.
\end{itemize}
We can now relate bulk mass fraction with bulk concentration directly,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bulkmassfraction}
		  \mathcal{X} = \frac{\phi_l \rho_l c_{l} + \phi_s \rho_s c_s}{\phi_l \rho_l + \phi_s \rho_s} 
		  = \phi_l c_{l} + \phi_s c_{s} = C = \phi_2 + \phi_l c_l.
\end{equation}

We relate bulk specific enthalpy with temperature and latent heat again.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:enthalpy}
		  H = T + \phi_l L/c_p.
\end{equation}
We further rescale by dividing melting temperature $\Delta T = T_{m0} - T_{e0}$ 
at standard atmospheric pressure 
forming a dimensionless relationship,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:enthalpydimensionless}
\check{H} = \check{T} + \phi_l \check{L},
\end{equation}
where $\check{L} = \frac{L}{c_p \Delta T}$ is the dimensionless latent heat.

\subsection{Eutectic Phase Split}
For simplicity, we are now assuming a linear eutectic phase split between different phase regions.
We can draw the eutectic phase diagram separating different phase regions with 
straight lines at a fixed pressure.
\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering\small
\includegraphics[width=5.5cm,height=4cm]{chapters/pics/eutectic.png}
\caption{Linear Eutectic Phase Spliting. \label{fig:Phase}}
\end{figure}
We now describe all six phase regions depicting in the phase split diagram above,
\begin{enumerate}[I]
\item Single-phase sub-solidus    : solid$1$
\item Two-phase sub-solidus       : solid$1$ + solid$2$
\item Three-phase eutectic        : solid$1$ + solid$2$ + melt
\item Two-phase super-eutectic    : solid$1$ + melt
\item Two-phase super-eutectic    : solid$2$ + melt
\item Single-phase super-liquidus : melt
\end{enumerate}
In the following discussion, we will restrict all the discussion to
a fixed pressure.

Region I. Pure component $1$. There is no eutectic phase behavior in this region.
The conditions to be in region I are
\begin{equation}
		  C = 0 \quad \check{H} \leq \check{T}_m,
\end{equation}
and we can determine phase fraction, mass composition and current temperature,
\begin{equation}
\phi_{1} = 1, \quad \phi_{2} = \phi_l = 0 \quad \text{and} 
		  \quad \check{T} = \check{H}.
\end{equation}
Region II. Sub-solidus: two components coexist as solid phase.
In this phase region, temperature hasn't reached eutectic melting temperature.
The conditions to be in region II are
\begin{equation}
		  C \neq 0 \quad \check{H} \leq \check{T}_e.
\end{equation}
We can determine phase fraction, mass composition and current temperature,
\begin{equation}
\phi_{2} = C \quad \phi_1 = 1-\phi_2 
		  \quad \phi_l = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad 
		  \check{T} = \check{H} .
\end{equation}
Region III. Two-phase eutectic: The system starts melting at eutectic temperature.
Both solid component $1$ and component $2$ and melt coexist at the same time.
Temperature fixs until component $2$ exhausts leaving eutectic melting.
Mass composition of component $2$ in the melt is also fixed as $\mathcal{X}_e$.
The conditions to be in region III are
%\begin{equation}
%		  C \leq \mathcal{X}_e \quad 
%		  \check{H} \leq \check{T}_e + \mathcal{X}_e \check{L}
%\end{equation}
We can determine phase fraction, mass composition and current temperature,
\begin{equation}
		  \phi_l = \frac{\check{H} - \check{T}_e}{\check{L}} \quad
		  \phi_2 = C - \phi_l \mathcal{X}_e \quad
		  \phi_1 = 1- \phi_l - \phi_2 \quad
		  \text{and} \quad \check{T} = \check{T}_e.
\end{equation}
Region IV. Super-eutectic two phase region. All component $2$ in solid has
been exhausted. The mass composition of component $2$ 
in liquid $c_{f}$ is now approximated with
a relation between mass fraction and temperature.
Subscript indicating component $2$ has been dropped since only one component 
is being tracked.
%The conditions to be in region IV are

%\end{equation}
We can determine phase fraction first,
\begin{equation}
    \phi_2 = 0 \quad \phi_l = \frac{C}{c_l(\check{T})}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding temperature should be determined by solving the following equation.
\begin{equation}
F(\check{T}) = \check{H} - \check{T} - 
		  \frac{C}{c_l(\check{T})} \check{L} = 0. 
\end{equation}
We can solve this nonlinear function with Newton iteration. However, we are 
approximating with a linear relationship 
$c_l = \mathcal{X}_e (\check{T}_m - \check{T}))$
where $c_l > C$ is imposed for the fact that there is component $1$ remaining as 
solid. Now we can solve a simple quadratic equation,
\begin{equation}
		  \check{T}^2 - (\check{H} + \check{T}_m)\check{T} + 
		  \check{T}_m \check{H} - C L/\mathcal{X}_e = 0.
\end{equation}
Omitting the root that exceeding melting temperature,
\begin{equation}
		  \begin{aligned}
		  \check{T} &= \frac{\check{H} + \check{T}_m - 
		  \sqrt{ (\check{H} + \check{T}_m)^2 - 
		  4(\check{T}_m \check{H} - C L/\mathcal{X}_e)} }{2} \\
					  &= 
		  \frac{\check{H} + \check{T}_m - 
		  \sqrt{ (\check{H} - \check{T}_m)^2 + 
		  4C L/\mathcal{X}_e} }{2} 
		  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Nondimensionalization}
Compaction and melting are two most important aspects in mantle dynamics.
We nondimensionlize the scaled formulation with characteristic length scale related to 
compaction length and scaled characteristic Darcy speed of the melt,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:physcalscales}
l_0 = \big( \frac{k_0 \mu_s}{\mu_l} \big)^{1/2} \quad\text{and}\quad u_0 = \frac{k_0 \rho_r g}{\mu_l}.
\end{equation}
We can derive corresponding characteristic time scale as $t_0 = l_0/u_0$, now we can write full set of
dimensionless equations. We represent dimensionless variables with a $ \check{}$ mark,
\begin{alignat}{2}
\label{eq:dimensionlessdarcy_flow_sc}
		  &\check{\mathbf{v}}_r
		  + \phi^{1+\Theta}\nabla(\phi^{-1/2}\check{q}_l)
        &&= 0,\\
\label{eq:dimensionlessconsMass_flow_sc}
		  &\phi^{-1/2}\nabla \cdot(\phi^{1+\Theta}\check{\mathbf{v}}_r)
        + \frac{1}{1-\phi}(\check{q}_l - \phi^{1/2}\check{q}) &&=0,\\
\label{eq:dimensionlessstokes_flow_sc}
		  &\nabla \check{q} - \nabla \cdot \check{\tau}(\check{\mathbf{v}}_s) &&= -(1-\phi)\mathbf{n},\\
\label{eq:dimensionlesscompaction_flow_sc}
		  &\nabla \cdot \check{\mathbf{v}}_s
        - \frac{\phi^{1/2}}{1-\phi}(\check{q}_l - \phi^{1/2}\check{q}) &&=0,
\end{alignat}
where dimensionless deviatoric stress of the solid phase is 
$\check{\tau} = 2(1-\phi)(\mathcal{D} \check{\mathbf{v}}_s - 1/3\nabla \cdot \check{\mathbf{v}}_s \mathbf{I})$ and 
$\mathbf{n}$ is the unit direction vector pointing downwards. Now we align transport equations with dimensionless 
variables defined in mechanic system,
\begin{equation}\label{dimensionlesstransport}
		  \begin{aligned}
					 \frac{\partial C}{\partial \check{t}} + \nabla \cdot (\check{\mathbf{v}}_{\text{eff}} C - \frac{\phi \mathcal{D}_l}{u_0l_0} \nabla c_l) &= 0,\\
					 \frac{\partial \check{H}}{\partial \check{t}} + \nabla \cdot \check{\overline{\mathbf{v}}} T - 
					 \check{L} \nabla \cdot \phi_s \check{\mathbf{v}}_s - \frac{\kappa}{u_0l_0} \nabla^2 \check{T} -  
					 \frac{\alpha_\rho l_0}{c_p} \check{T} \check{\overline{\mathbf{v}}} \cdot \mathbf{g} &= 0.
		  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
