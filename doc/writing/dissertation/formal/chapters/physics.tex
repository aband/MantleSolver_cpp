\section{Mechanics in Mantle Dynamics}
Extensive studies have been conducted on fluid dynamics of a two-phase mixture.
We briefly disscuss the four governing equations of mechanics, 
drawing on a
similar discussion presented by \citet{AveragedEquationsTwoPhase}, 
\citet{MagmaPhysics} and \citet{DynamicsPartiallyMoltenRock}.

\subsection{Phase Fraction}
We begin with defining the phase fraction using an indicator function, 
under the assumption that multiple phases cannot coexist at the same spatial location. 
Accordingly, the phases are considered immiscible.
The indicator function is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:indicatorfunction}
		  i(\mathbf{x}) = \left \{ 
		  \begin{aligned}
					 &1 \quad \text{phase}_i,\\
					 &0 \quad \text{not phase}_i.
		  \end{aligned}
		  \right.
\end{equation}
We then define the macroscopic phase fraction function of phase $i$ within a representative volume
element $V$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:porosityfunction}
		  \phi_i = \frac{1}{V} 
		  \int_{V} i(\mathbf{x}) d\mathbf{v},
\end{equation}
where $\sum_{i} \phi_i = 1$. In imcompressible setting, 
the phase fraction may be equivalently 
regarded as the volume fraction occupied by each phase within a 
representative elementary volume, reflecting the proportion of space 
occupied by each phase at the macroscopic scale. 
Phase-averaged (or volume-averaged) quantities, 
also referred to as bulk quantities, are defined as follows
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bulkquantity}
		  \overline{\gamma}  = \sum_{i} \phi_i \gamma_i,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_i$ denotes a quantity that may be scalar- or vector-valued. 

\subsection{Conservation Laws}
We consider the conservation equation for a single-phase system. 
For a given volumetric quantity $\gamma$ defined within 
a representative volume element $V$,
we can write a corresponding conservation equation as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generalconservationlaw}
		  \frac{d}{dt} \int_{V} \gamma d\mathbf{v} = 
		  - \int_{\partial V} \mathbf{f} \cdot d\sigma + \int_{V} S d\mathbf{v},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{f}$ represents flux travelling across the boundary of a representative 
volume element $V$. The minus sign indicates that the positive direction aligns with
normal unit vector pointing outwards. $S$, which is source/sink term, represents the volumetric
production rate of the volumetric quantity $\gamma$.
We apply Gauss's theorem to obtain differential form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conservationlawpde}
\frac{\partial \gamma}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{f} = S.
\end{equation}
We model the partially molten mantle as a two-phase mixture consisting of liquid magma
and solid mantle. The phase fraction of liquid magam is denoted by $\phi_l$ and phase
fraction of solid mantle is labelled as $\phi_s$, such that the relation $\phi_l + \phi_s = 1$ is
always attained.

We now rewrite the single-phase conservation law~\eqref{eq:conservationlawpde} 
with the bulk density defined 
in~\eqref{eq:bulkquantity} as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:massconservation}
\frac{\partial \overline{\rho}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\rho \mathbf{v}} = 0,
\end{equation}
where $\overline{\rho} = \phi_l \rho_l + \phi_s \rho_s$.
Under the Boussinesq approximation, we neglect the compressibility of each 
individual phase. Furthermore, we extend the Boussinesq approximation 
by disregarding 
the density difference between the two phases, except in the body force term. 
Accordingly, the continuity equation for the two-phase mixture can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuousfunction}
\nabla \cdot \overline{\mathbf{v}} = 0.
\end{equation}

Similarly, the conservation equation for the phase-averaged momentum takes 
the form of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:momentumconservation}
\frac{\partial \overline{\rho \mathbf{v}}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\rho \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{v}}  = 
\overline{\rho}\mathbf{g} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\pmb{\tau}} - \nabla \overline{p},
\end{equation}
where $\pmb{\tau}$ represents deviatoric stress.
By neglecting inertial effects, justified by the low Reynolds number of magma 
and mantle, the static force balance equation reduces to:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:staticmomentumconservation}
\overline{\rho}\mathbf{g} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\pmb{\sigma}}= 0,
\end{equation}
where $\pmb{\sigma} = \pmb{\tau} - p\pmb{I}$ represents the total stress, 
consisting of the deviatoric component $\pmb{\tau}$ and the isotropic 
pressure $p\pmb{I}$.

\subsection{Darcy's Law}
The equations governing bulk quantities~\eqref{eq:continuousfunction} 
and~\eqref{eq:staticmomentumconservation} alone are insufficient to simultaneously
determine the two distinct velocities and pressures. Therefore, an additional 
constitutive relationship is required to describe the behavior of the liquid 
phase within the porous medium.
We now examine more closely the interphase forces between the solid and liquid 
phases. A brief overview of the derivation of the individual governing equations 
for each phase is provided here, while more detailed and rigorous treatments can 
be found in the mixture theory literature, such as~\citet{mixturetheory}.
We write momentum balances for each individual phase as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phasemomentum}
\begin{aligned}
		  0 &= \phi_l \rho_l \mathbf{g} + \nabla \cdot (\phi_l \pmb{\tau}_l) - \nabla (\phi_l p_l) + \mathbf{F}_l,\\
		  0 &=  \phi_s \rho_s \mathbf{g} + \nabla \cdot (\phi_s \pmb{\tau}_s) - \nabla (\phi_s p_s) - \mathbf{F}_s,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{F}_l = \mathbf{F}_s$ are the interphase forces between solid and 
liquid phases. 
The interphase forces between the solid and liquid phases are equal and 
opposite, in accordance with Newton’s third law.
Neglecting the deviatoric stress in the liquid phase and retaining only the 
isotropic pressure component, we balance the remaining stress with the viscous 
drag force and the equilibrium body force.
Accordingly, we obtain the following relation for the relative velocity between 
phases, in alignment with Darcy’s law
\begin{equation}\label{eq:darcylaw}
		  \phi_l (\mathbf{v}_l - \mathbf{v}_s)
		  + \frac{k_{\phi_l}}{\mu_l}(\nabla p_l - \rho_f\mathbf{g})
	    = 0,
\end{equation}
where $k_{\phi_l}$ is the matrix permeability, which usually takes the quadratic 
form as $k_{\phi_l} = k_0 \phi_l^2$.

\subsection{Compaction}
In the context of mantle dynamics, an additional yet unique and essential
relationship regulating melt migration. 
We now revisit the continuous equation~\eqref{eq:continuousfunction},
considering the two phases separately and incorporating the effects of melting
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuousequationPhase}
		  \begin{aligned}
					 \frac{\partial \phi_l }{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\phi_l 
					 \mathbf{v}_l) &= \Gamma, \\
					 \frac{\partial (1-\phi_l) }{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot ((1-\phi_l)
					 \mathbf{v}_s) &= -\Gamma,
		  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma$ is the unknown melting rate. We now focus on the solid phase 
equation to get the evolving compaction relationship as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solidmelting}
		  \frac{\partial \phi_l}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v}_s \cdot \nabla \phi_l = 
		  \Gamma + (1-\phi_l) \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s,
\end{equation}
where the divergence term $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s$ is often referred to as the 
compaction rate.
We approximate the static compaction relation as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compactionviscosity}
		  \xi_{\phi_l}\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s  =p_l - p_s, 
\end{equation}
where $\xi_{\phi_l}$ is often referred to as bulk viscosity or 
compaction viscosity.
The form of the compaction viscosity $\xi_{\phi_l}$ has been the subject of extensive discussion. 
\citet{MagmaPhysics} popularized the concept of
compaction viscosity and relating the term to the stress balance in partially
molten system.
\citet{TwoPhaseModelCompaction} extended this formulation by incorporating 
surface tension effects. 
\citet{Compactioninamantlewithaverysmallmelt} further examined compaction behavior 
in the limit of infinitesimally small porosity. 
Following the approaches of~\citet{PartialMeltinginUpwellingMantleColumn} 
and~\citet{MagmaDynamicsEnthalpyMethod}, we approximate the compaction
viscosity $\xi_\phi$ 
using a simplified constitutive relationship,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bulkviscosity}
\xi_\phi = \frac{\mu_s}{\phi},
\end{equation}
which highlights the fact that increased melting facilitates the compaction of the
solid phase, while in the limit of no melting, 
compaction is entirely eliminated.

\subsection{Full Static System}
By combining equations~\eqref{eq:continuousfunction},~\eqref{eq:staticmomentumconservation},~\eqref{eq:darcylaw} 
and~\eqref{eq:compactionviscosity},
We finally arive with the full static flow mechanical system,
\begin{alignat}{2}
    \label{eq:darcy}
		  &\phi_l (\mathbf{v}_l - \mathbf{v}_s)
		  + \frac{k_{\phi_l}}{\mu_l}(\nabla p_l - \rho_f\mathbf{g})
	    &&= 0,\\
	\label{eq:continuous}
		  &\nabla \cdot (\phi_l\mathbf{v}_l + \phi_s\mathbf{v}_s) &&=0,\\
	\label{eq:stokes}
		  &\nabla\bar p - \nabla \cdot \pmb{\tau}(\mathbf{v}_s) + \overline{\rho}\mathbf{g}&&= 0,\\
	\label{eq:compaction}
		  &\mu_s\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s - \phi_l(p_l - p_s) &&=0, 
\end{alignat}
where the deviatoric stress of the solid is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deviatoricstress}
		  \pmb{\tau}(\mathbf{v}_s)
		  = 2\mu_s\phi_s\big(\mathcal{D} \mathbf{v}_s - \tfrac{1}{3}\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s \mathbf{I}\big),
\end{equation}
and $\mathcal{D} \mathbf{v}_s=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{v}_s + \nabla\mathbf{v}_s^T)$ is the symmetric gradient.
Moreover, we adjust the relative permeability 
$k_{\phi_l} = k_0 \phi_l^{2+2\Theta}$ with as constant 
$\Theta \in [0,1/2]$ . $\mu_s$ 
and $\mu_l$ are dynamic viscosities for solid and liquid correspondingly.
This complete static mechanical system relates the velocities and pressures of 
the solid and liquid phases to a given porosity distribution, 
thereby laying the foundation for subsequent computations.

\subsection{Scaled Formulation}
The Darcy equation will mathematically degenerate in the regions where 
the porosity is zero.
To handle this issue, we use the formulation developed 
previously by~\citet{DegenerateDarcy}. We first define pressure potentials as
\begin{equation}
	q_l = p_l - \rho_l g z\quad\text{and}\quad q_s = p_s - \rho_l g z,
\end{equation}
where $g=|\mathbf{g}|$ is the magnitude of gravity acceleration and $z$ is the 
depth, such that $\mathbf{g}=g\nabla z$. Note that $\rho_l$ is used to define 
both potentials, so that with $q=\bar q = \bar p - \rho_l g z$,
\begin{equation*}
	p_l - p_s = q_l - q_s = \frac{1}{1-\phi_l}(q_l - q).
\end{equation*}

In the mechanical system, the subscript on 
$\phi_l$ will be omitted.
and we will simply write it as $\phi$.
Accordingly, the solid phase fraction will be expressed as $1 - \phi$, 
emphasizing the constraint that the two phase fractions sum to unit,
\begin{alignat}{2}
		  &\phi\mathbf{v}_r + \frac{k_0\phi^{2+2\Theta}}{\mu_l}\nabla q_l &&= 0,\\
		  &\mu_s\nabla \cdot (\phi\mathbf{v}_r) + \frac{\phi}{1-\phi}(q_l-q) &&=0,\\
  		  &\nabla q - \nabla \cdot \tau(\mathbf{v}_s) &&= -(1-\phi)\rho_r\mathbf{g},\\
		  &\mu_s\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s - \frac{\phi}{1-\phi}(q_l-q) &&=0,
\end{alignat}
where relative velocity $\mathbf{v}_r = \mathbf{v}_l - \mathbf{v}_s$ and 
density difference 
$\rho_r = \rho_s - \rho_l$. 
Scaled variables are also defined,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scaledVariables}
		  \tilde{\mathbf{v}}_r = \phi^{-\Theta}\mathbf{v}_r
	\quad\text{and}\quad
	\tilde{q}_l = \phi^{1/2}q_l,
\end{equation}
and the problem is reformulated as
\begin{alignat}{2}
\label{eq:darcy_flow_sc}
		  &\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_r
		  + \frac{k_0\phi^{1+\Theta}}{\mu_f}\nabla(\phi^{-1/2}\tilde{q}_l)
        &&= 0,\\
\label{eq:consMass_flow_sc}
		  &\mu_s\phi^{-1/2}\nabla \cdot(\phi^{1+\Theta}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_r)
        + \frac{1}{1-\phi}(\tilde{q}_l - \phi^{1/2}q) &&=0,\\
\label{eq:stokes_flow_sc}
		  &\nabla q - \nabla \cdot \tau(\mathbf{v}_s) &&= -(1-\phi)\rho_r \mathbf{g},\\
\label{eq:compaction_flow_sc}
		  &\mu_s\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_s
        - \frac{\phi^{1/2}}{1-\phi}(\tilde{q}_l - \phi^{1/2}q) &&=0,
\end{alignat}
where the new scaled relative velocity maintain numerical stability in zero prosority region.

\section{Transport of Chemical Species and Energy}
With the velocities of solid and liquid phases determined, 
we are able to
evolve our system forward in time.
However, the realistic chemical compositions of mantle and magma is highly
complicated and very hard to trace at the same time. 
Therefore, we present a simple two-species two-phase model. 
In this framework, we also negelect detailed description of chemical reaction 
process and excluding the effect of the change of Gibbs free energies. 
Similar discussion can be found in extensive literatures like 
\citet{ConsequencesMeltTransport}, \citet{PartialMeltinginUpwellingMantleColumn}, \citet{MagmaDynamicsEnthalpyMethod} 
and \citet{DownwardOxidantTransport}.
Both species can exist in either the solid or liquid phase. They are assumed to 
be immiscible in the solid state but fully miscible in the liquid phase.
By again extending the Boussinesq approximation, we consider a homogenous density
$\rho$ for both solid phase and liquid phase. Figure~\ref{fig:RVE} illustrates 
the coexistence of species and phases in an representative volume element.

Within the representative volume element $V$, $\gamma_{i,j}$ represents one quantity of the species $i$ presenting in
phase $j$, where $i \in {1,2}$ and $j \in \{s,l\}$.   
The mass fraction $c_{i,j}$ of a given species $i$ in phase $j$ is also regarded 
as concentration.
We can write transport equations for a individual species $i$ assuming a pseudo melting term $\Gamma$ representing the transition between solid phase 
and liquid phase,
\begin{alignat}{1}
\label{eq:conservationliquidindividual}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big( \phi_l \rho_l c_{i,l} \big)  + \nabla \cdot \big(\phi_l \rho_l c_{i,l} \mathbf{v}_l - \phi_l \rho_l \mathcal{D}_{i,l} \nabla c_{i,l} \big) &= \Gamma ,\\
\label{eq:conservationsolidindividual}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big( \phi_s \rho_s c_{i,s} \big)  + \nabla \cdot \big(\phi_s \rho_l c_{i,s} \mathbf{v}_s - \phi_s \rho_s \mathcal{D}_{i,s} \nabla c_{i,s} \big) &= -\Gamma ,
\end{alignat}
where the diffusion term is dropped in solid phase.
By summing up equation~\eqref{eq:conservationliquidindividual} and 
equation~\eqref{eq:conservationsolidindividual},
we obtain an advection-diffusion equation expressed in terms of the 
phase-averaged volumetric bulk variable
\begin{equation} \label{eq:specietransport}
		  \frac{\partial \overline{\rho c_{i}}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \big( \overline{\rho c_{i} \mathbf{v}} - \phi_l \rho_l \mathcal{D}_{i,l} \nabla c_{i,l} \big) = 0,
\end{equation}
where the phase averaged quantities are defined as 
$\overline{\rho c_i} = \sum_{j\in \{s,l\}} \phi_j \rho_j c_{i,j}$ 
and $\overline{\rho c_i \mathbf{v}} = \sum_{j\in \{s,l\}} \phi_j \rho_j c_{i,j} \mathbf{v}_j$.
The subscript $i$ will be omitted, as only one chemical species needs to be
explicitly computed
in this two-species system.
By extending the Boussinessq approximation such that 
$\rho_l = \rho_s = \overline{\rho}$, the 
species transport equation can be simplified for the following form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:specietransportsimplified}
\frac{\partial \overline{c}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \big( \overline{ c \mathbf{v}} - \phi_l \mathcal{D}_{l} \nabla c_l \big) = 0.
\end{equation}

We write energy balance equation in the phase-averaged internal energy form first,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energybalanceinternalenergy}
\frac{\partial \overline{\rho u}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\rho u \mathbf{v}} - \nabla \cdot \overline{K} \nabla T = 
		  \overline{\rho \mathcal{Q}} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\pmb{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{v}} + \overline{\rho \mathbf{v}} \cdot \mathbf{g}.
\end{equation}
On the left-hand side, the equation contains the time-dependent rate of change 
of internal energy, along with advection and diffusion terms.
On the right-hand side, it includes the heat source term $\mathcal{Q}$, mechanical work done by stress, and work against gravity. 
In a thermochemical equilibrium scenerio, we replace internal energy with 
enthalpy. We formulate the energy balance equation by neglecting 
external heating/cooling term $\mathcal{Q}$ and dissipative heating 
following the discussion presented by \citet{MagmaDynamicsEnthalpyMethod},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energyconservationenthalpy}
\frac{\partial \overline{\rho h}}{\partial t} + \rho c_p \nabla \cdot \overline{\mathbf{v}} T = 
\rho L \nabla \cdot \phi_s \mathbf{v}_s + \overline{K} \nabla^2 T + \rho \alpha_\rho T \overline{\mathbf{v}} \cdot \mathbf{g},
\end{equation}
where the specific heat capacity $c_p$ is used for both 
components in solid and liquid phase.
The last term including gravitational acceleration has previously been referred 
to as adiabatic cooling or decompression cooling by 
\citet{ExtensionofLithosphere}.
We divide $\rho c_p$ on both sides of the equation 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalizedenthalpy}
		  \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \overline{\mathbf{v}} T = 
		  \frac{L}{c_p} \nabla \cdot \phi_s \mathbf{v}_s + \kappa \nabla^2 T + 
		  \frac{\alpha_\rho}{c_p} T \overline{\mathbf{v}} \cdot \mathbf{g},
\end{equation}
where $H = \overline{h}/c_p$ is the bulk specific enthalpy and $\kappa = 
\overline{K}/\rho c_p$ is the heat diffusivity.

\subsection{Effective Velocity}
The concept of effective velocity has long been employed in chemistry, 
particularly in the context of particle transport. In mantle dynamics, 
\citet{GeochemicalConsequence} applied effective velocity for equilibrium 
transport of concentration within the liquid phase. We define a 
corresponding effective velocity for the transport of bulk composition.
To begin, we define the partition coefficient as $D_i = c_{i,s}/c_{i,f}$. 
The concentration of species $i$ in the liquid and solid phase 
can be expressed in terms of the bulk composition as follows:
\begin{alignat}{1}
		  \label{eq:effectiveliquidcon}
		  (\phi_l + \phi_s D_i) c_{i,l} &= \overline{c}_i, \\
		  \label{eq:effectivesolidcon}
		  (\phi_l/D_i + \phi_s) c_{i,s} &= \overline{c}_i. 
\end{alignat}
By expressing $c_{i,l}$ and $c_{i,s}$ with bulk composition given by 
equations~\eqref{eq:effectiveliquidcon} and~\eqref{eq:effectivesolidcon}, 
we can rewrite the bulk velocity as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:effectivevel}
		  \overline{c_{i}\bold{v}} = \phi_s \bold{v}_s c_{i,s} + \phi_l \bold{v}_l c_{i,l} = 
		  \bigg( \frac{\phi_l \bold{v}_l + D\phi_s \bold{v}_s}{\phi_l + \phi_s D} \bigg) \overline{c}_i.
\end{equation}
We get the proper expression for the effective velocity, denoted as 
$\bold{v}_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\phi_l \bold{v}_l + D_i\phi_s \bold{v}_s}{\phi_l + \phi_s D_i}$, 
which is the appropriate velocity bulk composition should advecting.
In the absence of melting, where $\phi_l = 0$, we reterive 
$\mathbf{v}_{\text{eff}} = \mathbf{v}_s$.
Conversly, in the case of complete melting, where $\phi_l = 1$, we obtain
$\mathbf{v}_{\text{eff}} = \mathbf{v}_l$.
Therefore, the definition of effective velocity is well defined at those two boundary scenerios. 
We rewrite the transport equation of species with effective velocity~\eqref{eq:effectivevel} and rewrite $\overline{c}$ with $C$ for simplicity as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:effectivetransportspecie}
		  \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = 
		  \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{v}_{\text{eff}} C - \phi_l \mathcal{D}_l \nabla c_l)
		  = 0.
\end{equation}

\section{Eutectic Phase Behavior}
With the transport equations defined, we can now proceed to redistribute 
chemical species and energy within the system. The next step is to determine 
the thermodynamic state of the system, assuming thermodynamic equilibrium is 
attained at each time step. 
Recognizing that mid-ocean ridge forms in regions where temperature 
are generally lower, eutectic melting, where the presence of a secondary species 
significantly lowers the melting temperature, provides a suitable model for 
describing the melting behavior in these areas.

The binary eutectic system has been widely applied in studies involving
chemical equilibrium, with detailed
description can be found in textbook such as book by~\citet{Chemistry}.
In the context of mantle dynamics, eutectic melting was being incorporated 
in work by~\citet{GenerationCompositionPartialMelts}.
Moreover, eutectic phase behavior has been applied to solidification of 
alloys by~\citet{eutecticsolidification}.
More recently, binary eutectic melting has also
been applied to water-salt-brine system in glacier, as shown 
by~\citet{DownwardOxidantTransport}. 

We assume the two primary components in our partially molten system are olivine and orthopyroxene, 
which are highly miscible in the liquid phase, but immiscible in the solid phase.
Olivine is considered the dominant component, while orthopyroxene represents the 
minor component.
Assuming thermodynamic equilibrium is attained at each time step, 
we can determine phase fraction of liquid, volume fraction of each solid
components and temperature on a binary eutectic phase diagram with
transported enthalpy and bulk composition.

\subsection{Clausis-Clapeyron Relation}
In geological contexts, pressure can undergo significant variations during 
dynamic events such as mantle upwelling. As a result, the influence of 
pressure on the melting point cannot be neglected.
To account for this, we approximate both the solidus and eutectic temperatures 
at a given pressure using the approximated Clausius–Clapeyron relationship.
We assume a simple relation between as 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calusiusclapeyron}
\begin{aligned}
		  T_m &= T_{m0} + \nu^{-1} (P-P_0), \\
		  T_e &= T_{e0} + \nu^{-1} (P-P_0), \\
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\nu$ is the Clausius-Clapeyron constant, and
$P_0$ is the reference pressure usually set to be 0.
The melting point $T_{m0}$ and eutectic point $T_{e0}$ 
refer to their respective values at standard atmospheric pressure.

\subsection{Some Assumptions}
In a eutectic system, the current thermodynamic state of the material can be identified using the bulk mass fraction of opx (orthopyroxene) 
and bulk enthalpy of the system. 
Let $\mathcal{X}_i$ represents the bulk mass fraction of species $i$ 
and $\mathcal{X}_{i,e}$ denotes mass fraction of specie $i$ in liquid at 
the eutectic point, which is a constant.
The subscript $i$ will be omitted in the later context, as we consider a binary
system involving only two components.

We begin by introducing some assumptions regarding densities and other material 
proterties.
%\begin{itemize}
%\item Assuming thermodynamic equilibrium is achieved at every time step that phase state can be calculated
%		  with phase diagram.
%\item The composition of the second specie in the liquid formed by eutectic melting $\mathcal{X}_e$ is constant.  
%\item Melting temperature $T_m$ and eutectic temperature $T_e$ are functions to pressure $T_m(P)$ and $T_e(P)$. 
%\item Boussinessq approximation is extended to phase behavior as well $\rho_l = \rho_s$.
%\item Assuming no density difference between two solid components.
%\item Assuming constant thermal expansion and specific heat capacity.
%\end{itemize}
\begin{itemize}
		  \item We assume no density difference between solid phase olivine and opx
					 i.e. 
					 $\rho_{\text{opx}} = \rho_{\text{olivine}}$.
		  \item We assume no density change during phase transition 
					 (extended Boussinessq approximation) i.e. 
					 $\rho_l = \rho_s$.
		  \item We assume constant and uniform material thermodynamics properties 
					 like
					 specific heat capacity and thermal expansion coefficient.
\end{itemize}
Following the assumptions, we express the bulk composition according to its 
definition as follows
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bulkmassfraction}
		  \mathcal{X} = \frac{\phi_l \rho_l c_{l} + \phi_s \rho_s c_s}{\phi_l \rho_l + \phi_s \rho_s} 
		  = \phi_l c_{l} + \phi_s c_{s} = C = \phi_2 + \phi_l c_l.
\end{equation}
We also express bulk specific enthalpy with temperature and latent heat 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:enthalpy}
		  H = T + \phi_l L/c_p.
\end{equation}
According to equaions~\eqref{eq:calusiusclapeyron}, the difference between
melting point and eutectic point, defined as
$\Delta T = T_m - T_e = T_{m0} - T_{e0}$, which remains constant regardless of
pressure change. At any given pressure,
we proceed by subtracting $T_e$ from both side of the equation, and dividing 
this $\Delta T$ get a renormalized dimensionless formulation as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:enthalpydimensionless}
		  \frac{H-T_m}{\Delta T} = \frac{T-T_m}{\Delta T} + 
		  \frac{\phi_l L}{c_p \Delta T}.
\end{equation}
Let $\check{L} \coloneq \frac{L}{c_p \Delta T}$, 
$\check{H} \coloneq \frac{H-T_m}{\Delta T}$ 
and $\check{T} \coloneq \frac{T-T_m}{\Delta T}$. We rewrite the enthalpy 
temperature relationship as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:enthalpydimensionlessrenormalized}
		  \check{H} = \check{T} + \phi_l\check{L}. 
\end{equation}

\subsection{Phase Diagram}
Binary eutectic melting is usually highly-nonlinear.
For simplicity, we adopt a geometric parameterization of the binary 
eutectic diagram that can be analytically evaluated. 
We implement a linear eutectic phase boundary to distinguish between different
phase regions within the diagram.

We begin with a brief description of eutectic melting illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:Phase}.

We split phase diagram into six regions.
\begin{enumerate}[I]
\item Single-phase sub-solidus    : Olivine
\item Two-phase sub-solidus       : Olivine + Orthopyroxene
\item Three-phase eutectic        : Olivine + Orthopyroxene + melt
\item Two-phase super-eutectic    : Olivine + melt
\item Two-phase super-eutectic    : Orthopyroxene + melt
\item Single-phase super-liquidus : melt
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase Split}
Region I. Pure component $1$. There is no eutectic phase behavior in this region.
The conditions to be in region I are
\begin{equation}
		  C = 0 \quad \check{H} \leq \check{T}_m,
\end{equation}
and we can determine phase fraction, mass composition and current temperature,
\begin{equation}
\phi_{1} = 1, \quad \phi_{2} = \phi_l = 0 \quad \text{and} 
		  \quad \check{T} = \check{H}.
\end{equation}
Region II. Sub-solidus: two components coexist as solid phase.
In this phase region, temperature hasn't reached eutectic melting temperature.
The conditions to be in region II are
\begin{equation}
		  C \neq 0 \quad \check{H} \leq \check{T}_e.
\end{equation}
We can determine phase fraction, mass composition and current temperature,
\begin{equation}
\phi_{2} = C \quad \phi_1 = 1-\phi_2 
		  \quad \phi_l = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad 
		  \check{T} = \check{H} .
\end{equation}
Region III. Two-phase eutectic: The system starts melting at eutectic temperature.
Both solid component $1$ and component $2$ and melt coexist at the same time.
Temperature fixs until component $2$ exhausts leaving eutectic melting.
Mass composition of component $2$ in the melt is also fixed as $\mathcal{X}_e$.
The conditions to be in region III are
%\begin{equation}
%		  C \leq \mathcal{X}_e \quad 
%		  \check{H} \leq \check{T}_e + \mathcal{X}_e \check{L}
%\end{equation}
We can determine phase fraction, mass composition and current temperature,
\begin{equation}
		  \phi_l = \frac{\check{H} - \check{T}_e}{\check{L}} \quad
		  \phi_2 = C - \phi_l \mathcal{X}_e \quad
		  \phi_1 = 1- \phi_l - \phi_2 \quad
		  \text{and} \quad \check{T} = \check{T}_e.
\end{equation}
Region IV. Super-eutectic two phase region. All component $2$ in solid has
been exhausted. The mass composition of component $2$ 
in liquid $c_{f}$ is now approximated with
a relation between mass fraction and temperature.
Subscript indicating component $2$ has been dropped since only one component 
is being tracked.
%The conditions to be in region IV are
%\begin{equation}
%		  C \leq \mathcal{X}_e \quad
%		  \check{H} \leq \check{T}_e + \check{L}
%\end{equation}
We can determine phase fraction first,
\begin{equation}
    \phi_2 = 0 \quad \phi_l = \frac{C}{c_l(\check{T})}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding temperature should be determined by solving the following equation.
\begin{equation}
F(\check{T}) = \check{H} - \check{T} - 
		  \frac{C}{c_l(\check{T})} \check{L} = 0. 
\end{equation}
We can solve this nonlinear function with Newton iteration. However, we are 
approximating with a linear relationship 
$c_l = \mathcal{X}_e (\check{T}_m - \check{T}))$
where $c_l > C$ is imposed for the fact that there is component $1$ remaining as 
solid. Now we can solve a simple quadratic equation,
\begin{equation}
		  \check{T}^2 - (\check{H} + \check{T}_m)\check{T} + 
		  \check{T}_m \check{H} - C L/\mathcal{X}_e = 0.
\end{equation}
Omitting the root that exceeding melting temperature,
\begin{equation}
		  \begin{aligned}
		  \check{T} &= \frac{\check{H} + \check{T}_m - 
		  \sqrt{ (\check{H} + \check{T}_m)^2 - 
		  4(\check{T}_m \check{H} - C L/\mathcal{X}_e)} }{2} \\
					  &= 
		  \frac{\check{H} + \check{T}_m - 
		  \sqrt{ (\check{H} - \check{T}_m)^2 + 
		  4C L/\mathcal{X}_e} }{2} 
		  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Nondimensionalization}
Compaction and melting are two most important aspects in mantle dynamics.
We nondimensionlize the scaled formulation with characteristic length scale related to 
compaction length and scaled characteristic Darcy speed of the melt,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:physcalscales}
l_0 = \big( \frac{k_0 \mu_s}{\mu_l} \big)^{1/2} \quad\text{and}\quad u_0 = \frac{k_0 \rho_r g}{\mu_l}.
\end{equation}
We can derive corresponding characteristic time scale as $t_0 = l_0/u_0$, now we can write full set of
dimensionless equations. We represent dimensionless variables with a $ \check{}$ mark,
\begin{alignat}{2}
\label{eq:dimensionlessdarcy_flow_sc}
		  &\check{\mathbf{v}}_r
		  + \phi^{1+\Theta}\nabla(\phi^{-1/2}\check{q}_l)
        &&= 0,\\
\label{eq:dimensionlessconsMass_flow_sc}
		  &\phi^{-1/2}\nabla \cdot(\phi^{1+\Theta}\check{\mathbf{v}}_r)
        + \frac{1}{1-\phi}(\check{q}_l - \phi^{1/2}\check{q}) &&=0,\\
\label{eq:dimensionlessstokes_flow_sc}
		  &\nabla \check{q} - \nabla \cdot \check{\tau}(\check{\mathbf{v}}_s) &&= -(1-\phi)\mathbf{n},\\
\label{eq:dimensionlesscompaction_flow_sc}
		  &\nabla \cdot \check{\mathbf{v}}_s
        - \frac{\phi^{1/2}}{1-\phi}(\check{q}_l - \phi^{1/2}\check{q}) &&=0,
\end{alignat}
where dimensionless deviatoric stress of the solid phase is 
$\check{\tau} = 2(1-\phi)(\mathcal{D} \check{\mathbf{v}}_s - 1/3\nabla \cdot \check{\mathbf{v}}_s \mathbf{I})$ and 
$\mathbf{n}$ is the unit direction vector pointing downwards. Now we align transport equations with dimensionless 
variables defined in mechanic system,
\begin{equation}\label{dimensionlesstransport}
		  \begin{aligned}
					 \frac{\partial C}{\partial \check{t}} + \nabla \cdot (\check{\mathbf{v}}_{\text{eff}} C - \frac{\phi \mathcal{D}_l}{u_0l_0} \nabla c_l) &= 0,\\
					 \frac{\partial \check{H}}{\partial \check{t}} + \nabla \cdot \check{\overline{\mathbf{v}}} T - 
					 \check{L} \nabla \cdot \phi_s \check{\mathbf{v}}_s - \frac{\kappa}{u_0l_0} \nabla^2 \check{T} -  
					 \frac{\alpha_\rho l_0}{c_p} \check{T} \check{\overline{\mathbf{v}}} \cdot \mathbf{g} &= 0.
		  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
