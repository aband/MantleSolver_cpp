\section{Finite Volume Method}
The finite volume method (FVM) is a widely used discretization method for
various problems governed by conservation laws.
The FVM is particularly robust and well-suited for  
arbitrary complex geometry. 
A key advantage of FVM that benefits this simulation is
its natural enforcement of local mass conservation.

To begin with, we consider a general hyperbolic conservation law 
regarding a scalar variable $u$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conservationlaw}
		  \left \{
		  \begin{alignedat}{2}
					 &\frac{\partial u(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{f}(u; \mathbf{x},t) = 0, \hspace{0.2cm} t>0,\, &&\mathbf{x} \in \Omega, \\ 
					 &u(\mathbf{x},0) = u_0(\mathbf{x}), \hspace{2.5cm} &&\mathbf{x} \in \Omega,
		  \end{alignedat}
		  \right .
\end{equation}
where we assume the domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$. The term $\mathbf{f}$ represents
the flux transporting the scalar variable $u$.
The basic semi-discretized formulation of the finite volume method can be written as
\begin{equation}
		  \frac{\partial \overline{u}_E}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{\lvert E \rvert}\int_{\partial E} \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},t) \cdot \pmb{\nu}\, d\mathbf{x} = 0,
\end{equation}
where the cell-averaged value $\overline{u}$ is calculated as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cellaveval}
\overline{u}_E = \frac{1}{\lvert E \rvert} \int_{E} u(\mathbf{x}, t)\, 
		  d\mathbf{x},
\end{equation}
where $\lvert E \rvert$ denote the area of the cell $E$.
Here, $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},t)$ denotes the approximated numerical flux. 
We employ the Lax-Friedrichs numerical flux
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LFflux}
		  \mathbf{F}(u^+, u^-) = \frac{1}{2}\Big[\big(\mathbf{f}(u^+)+\mathbf{f}(u^-)\big)\cdot \pmb{\nu} - \alpha_{LF}(u^+-u^-)\Big],
\end{equation}
where $u^+$ and $u^-$ denote the values approximated from the perspective of element 
$E^+ \in \mathcal{T}_h$ and $E^- \in \mathcal{T}_h$ respectively, where
$E^+ \cap E^-$ is an edge of the mesh.
The unit normal vector $\pmb{\nu}$ points from $E^-$ to $E^+$, and is orthogonal to the edge.
The parameter $\alpha_{LF}$ is the Lax-Friedrichs stabilizer factor. 
Depending on the available information about the solution, there are two possible approaches for computing $\alpha_{LF}$:
\begin{itemize}
		  \item The global Lax-Friedrichs Stabilizer: 
					 \begin{equation*}
								\alpha_{LF} = \max_{\mathbf{x}\in \Omega,\, t>0,\, u\in \mathbb{R}}\bigg\lvert \frac{\partial \mathbf{f}(u;\mathbf{x},t)\cdot \pmb{\nu}}{\partial u} \bigg\rvert;
					 \end{equation*}
		  \item The local Lax-Friedrichs Stabilizer:
					 \begin{equation*}
								\alpha_{LF} = \max_{\text{edges}\,e}\Bigg(\bigg\lvert \frac{\partial \mathbf{f}(u^+;\mathbf{x},t) \cdot \pmb{\nu}}{\partial u}\Big\vert_e \bigg\rvert,
								\bigg\lvert \frac{\partial \mathbf{f}(u^-;\mathbf{x},t) \cdot \pmb{\nu}}{\partial u}\Big\vert_e \bigg\rvert \Bigg).
\end{equation*}
\end{itemize}
The numerical flux $\mathbf{F}$, as defined in equation~\eqref{eq:LFflux}, is consistent and conservative across element interfaces. 
We now replace the $u^+$ and $u^-$ with reconstructed values $\mathcal{R}^+$ and $\mathcal{R}^-$ and express the numerical flux as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reconstructionflux}
		  \mathbf{F}(\mathcal{R}^+, \mathcal{R}-) = \frac{1}{2}[(\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{R}^+)+\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{R}^-)\cdot \pmb{\nu} - \alpha_{LF}(\mathcal{R}^+-\mathcal{R}^-)].
\end{equation}
Here, the reconstructed value $\mathcal{R}$ is of higher-order accuracy. We evaluate the
integrals over each edge $e_i \in \partial E$ with a suitable quadrature rule. 
Let $\lvert e_i \rvert$ denote the length of edge $e_i$. 
Assuming a quadrature rule with $G$ point per edge, the semi-discretized formulation becomes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fvdis}
		  \frac{\partial \overline{u}_{E}}{\partial t} + \sum_{i=0}^4\frac{\lvert e_i \rvert}{\lvert E \rvert}
		  \sum_{g=0}^G \omega_{i,g} \mathbf{F}(\mathcal{R}_{i,g}^+, \mathcal{R}_{i,g}^-)\cdot \pmb{\nu}_i = 0,
\end{equation}
where $\omega_{i,g}$ represents the weight associated to the $g\textsuperscript{th}$ quadrature points on edge $e_i$.

\section[ML-WENO]{The Multi-level Weighted Essentially Non-Oscillatory (ML-WENO) Method}
We developed an accurate, versatile, robust and efficient finite volume 
multi level weighted essentially non oscillatory (ML-WENO) reconstruction
in~\cite{MultiLevelWeno}.
ML-WENO is primarily used to reconstruct 
values on both sides of the element interfaces.
It can also be applied to a general 
reconstruction of discontinuous variables, e.g., piece-wise constant discontinuous Darcy pressure.

\subsection{Stencil Polynomials}
Consider rectangular stencils $\mathcal{S} = \{E_{i,j},\, i\leq s,\, j\leq t\} \subset \mathcal{T}_h$ on a quasi uniform 
logically rectangular mesh, which covers the area $\Omega_{\mathcal{S}} = \cup_{E \in \mathcal{S}} E$.
For each stencil, we
construct a tensor product stencil polynomial 
$Q^{\mathcal{S}}_{s,t} \in \mathbb{Q}_{s-1,t-1}(\Omega_{\mathcal{S}})$. % \vcentcolon= \mathbb{P}_{s-1}(x) \otimes \mathbb{P}_{t-1}(y)$,
%where $s$ and $t$ equal to the stencil sizes in the 
%x- and y-directions, respectively.
The scaled tensor product stencil polynomial is expressed as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:stencilpolynomial}
		  Q^{\mathcal{S}}_{s,t}(x,y) = \sum_{p<s,\  q<t} c_{p,q} \Big(\frac{x - x_\mathcal{S}}{h_\mathcal{S}}\Big)^p 
		  \Big(\frac{y - y_\mathcal{S}}{h_\mathcal{S}}\Big)^q,
\end{equation}
where $(x_\mathcal{S},y_\mathcal{S})$ is any point in the stencil 
$\mathcal{S}$. We choose the stencil ``center" point: the intersection point of two diagonal lines joining top left, bottom right
and top right, bottom left corners.
Assuming that each cell $E_{i,j} \in \mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{T}_h$ is shape regular as in Definition~\ref{def:shaperegularity},
we define the stencil scale parameter $h_\mathcal{S} = 
\sum_{i\leq s, j\leq t}E_{i,j}/(s t)$ as the average diameter of all cells in the stencil. 
Introducing the normalized coordinates
\begin{equation}
\xi = \frac{x-x_\mathcal{S}}{h_\mathcal{S}},\quad \text{and} \quad \eta = \frac{y-y_\mathcal{S}}{h_\mathcal{S}}.
\end{equation}
We can rewrite equation~\eqref{eq:stencilpolynomial} in normalized coordinates as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stencilpolynomialscaled}
		  \hat{Q}^{\mathcal{S}}_{s,t}(\xi, \eta) = \sum_{p<s,\ q<t} c_{p,q} \xi^p \eta^q.
\end{equation}

The tensor product stencil polynomial satisfies the condition that the cell-averaged
value \eqref{eq:cellaveval} is preserved through integration over each cell as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stencilpolycoefficent}
		  \frac{1}{|E_{i,j}|} \iint_{E_{i,j}} Q^{\mathcal{S}}_{s,t}(x,y)\, dxdy = \overline{u}_{i,j}, \quad 
		  \forall E_{i,j} \in \mathcal{S},
\end{equation}
where $\overline{u}_{E_{i,j}}$ represents the cell-averaged scalar variable over cell $E_{i,j}$.

One way to construct a stencil polynomial $Q^\mathcal{S}_{s,t}$ is using basis polynomials.
Let 
$Q^{\mathcal{S}}_{s,t} = \sum_{i,j} \overline{u}_{i,j} \phi^{i,j}_{s,t}$,
where the basis polynomials $\phi^{i,j}_{s,t}$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:basispoly}
		  \frac{1}{\lvert E_{i',j'}\rvert } \iint_{E_{i',j'}} \phi^{i,j}_{s,t} (x,y)\, dxdy = 
		  %\frac{1}{\lvert \hat{E}_{k'}\rvert} \iint_{\hat{E}_{k'}}\hat{\phi}^k_{s,t}(\xi, \eta) d\xi d\eta = 
		  \left \{
					 \begin{aligned}
								&1 \quad  (i',j') = (i,j)\\
								&0 \quad  (i',j') \neq (i,j)
					 \end{aligned}
					 \right. 
					 ,\quad \forall E_{i',j'} \in \mathcal{S},
\end{equation}
which always leads to an $(s t) \times (s t)$ non-singular linear system.
We can write the basis polynomials in terms of normalized coordinates $\xi$ and $\eta$ as well, and
rewrite them as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalizedstencilpoly}
\hat{Q}^{\mathcal{S}}_{s,t}(\xi, \eta) = \sum_{i,j} \overline{u}_k \hat{\phi}_{s,t}^{i,j}(\xi, \eta).
\end{equation}
The approxmatibility of a tensor product polynomial is related to the associated complete polynomial 
, as discussed by~\cite{PolynomialApproximation}.
Given a bounded interpolation projection operator $\mathcal{Q}$, for all $\mathbf{x} \in E \in \mathcal{S}$, we have
\begin{equation}
\lvert D^\alpha u(\mathbf{x}) - D^\alpha \big(\mathcal{Q} u(\mathbf{x})\big) \rvert \leq \left \{ 
\begin{alignedat}{2}
&C h^{r-\lvert \alpha \rvert}  \quad && \text{if $u$ is smooth on the stencil},\\
&C && \text{otherwise},
\end{alignedat}
		  \right.
\end{equation}
where $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ is a two-dimensional multi-index, and $\lvert \alpha \rvert = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 \leq r$.
This theorem applies to our stencil polynomials~\eqref{eq:stencilpolynomial}.

\subsection{Stencil Polynomial Smoothness Indicators}
A smoothness indicator $\sigma$ is defined to quantify the
smoothness of the tensor product polynomial $Q^\mathcal{S}_{r,r}$ on the stencil $\mathcal{S}$. 
The classical approach by~\cite{JSSmoothness} and~\cite{FRIEDRICH1998194} defines $\sigma$ in analogue to 
a Sobolev seminorm as
\begin{equation}
		  \sigma_{JS} = \sum_{1\leq \lvert\alpha\rvert \leq r} 
		  \int\int_{E_0} \frac{h_\mathcal{S}^{2\lvert\alpha\rvert}}{\lvert E_0 \rvert} 
		  (\mathcal{D}^\alpha Q(x,y))^2 dxdy,
\end{equation}
where $E_0$ can be any cell $E \in \mathcal{S}$, and is usually referred to as
the target cell, i.e., the cell $E$ form witch $(x,y)$ is taken in $Q^\mathcal{S}_{r,r}(x,y)$.

%where $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ is a multi-index in two dimension, $\lvert \alpha \rvert = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2$, 
%and $\mathcal{D}$ is the derivative operator $\mathcal{D}^\alpha = \partial^{\lvert \alpha \rvert} / \partial x^{\alpha_1} \partial y^{\alpha_2}$.
The Jiang-Shu smoothness indicator can be computed utilizing the predefined basis polynomials~\eqref{eq:basispoly}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:JSsmoothnessIC}
\begin{aligned}
		  \sigma_{JS} &= \sum_{1\leq \lvert \alpha \rvert \leq r}  \frac{h_\mathcal{S}^{2\lvert\alpha\rvert}}{\lvert E_0 \rvert} 
		  \iint_{E_0} (\mathcal{D}^\alpha Q(x,y))^2 dx dy\\
					  &= \frac{h_\mathcal{S}^2}{\lvert E_0 \rvert}\sum_{1\leq \lvert \alpha \rvert \leq r} \iint_{\hat{E}_0} (\mathcal{D}^\alpha\hat{Q}(\xi, \eta))^2 d\xi d\eta \\
					  &= \frac{h_\mathcal{S}^2}{\lvert E_0 \rvert}\sum_{1\leq \lvert \alpha \rvert \leq r} \iint_{\hat{E}_0} \big( \sum_{ij} \overline{u}_{ij} \mathcal{D}^\alpha \hat{\phi}_{i,j}(\xi, \eta) \big)^2 d\xi d\eta \\
					  &= \frac{h_\mathcal{S}^2}{\lvert E_0 \rvert}\sum_{ij} \sum_{i'j'} \overline{u}_{ij} \overline{u}_{i'j'} \sum_{1\leq \lvert \alpha \rvert \leq r} 
					  \iint_{\hat{E}_{ij}} \mathcal{D}^\alpha \hat{\phi}_{ij}(\xi, \eta) 
					  \mathcal{D}^\alpha \hat{\phi}_{i'j'}(\xi, \eta) d\xi d\eta \\
					  &= \frac{h_\mathcal{S}^2}{\lvert E_0 \rvert}\sum_{ij} \sum_{i'j'} \overline{u}_{ij} \overline{u}_{i'j'} \sigma^{i'j'}_{ij},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the coefficient tensor $\sigma^{i'j'}_{ij}$ can be precomputed before the time-stepping loop, after the mesh is established.

Alternatively, we can compute the Jiang-Shu smoothness indicator directly using 
the polynomial coefficients formulation~\eqref{eq:stencilpolynomialscaled} as
%Recall the tensor product polynomial version~\eqref{eq:stencilpolynomialscaled},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cofformulation}
		  \begin{aligned}
					  \sigma_{JS} &= \frac{h_\mathcal{S}^2}{\lvert E_0 \rvert}\sum_{1\leq \lvert \alpha \rvert \leq r} \iint_{\hat{E}_0} (\mathcal{D}^\alpha\hat{Q}(\xi, \eta))^2 d\xi d\eta \\
					 &= \sum_{\lvert \gamma \rvert \leq r} \sum_{\lvert \beta \rvert \leq r} c_\gamma c_\beta 
					 \bigg(\sum_{1\leq \lvert \alpha \rvert \leq r}\frac{h^2_\mathcal{S}}{\lvert E_0 \rvert} \iint_{E_0} 
					 \mathcal{D}^\alpha \xi^\gamma \mathcal{D}^\alpha \eta^\beta \, d\xi d\eta \bigg)\\
					 &= \sum_{\lvert \gamma \rvert \leq r} \sum_{\lvert \beta \rvert \leq r} c_\gamma c_\beta \zeta_{\gamma, \beta},
		  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
which paves the way for further simplification in~\eqref{eq:polysigma} below.

The smoothness indicator exhibits the following convergence property:
there exists a constant $C > 0$ such that when $h \rightarrow 0^+$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smoothconvergence}
		  \sigma = \begin{cases}
					 Ch^2 + \mathcal{O} (h^3) \quad &\text{u is smooth on $\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}$}, \\
					 \Theta (1)  &\text{u is not smooth on $\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}$}.
		  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Actually $\sigma = O(1)$ in the nonsmooth case, but in practice we find that
$\sigma = \Theta(1)$. Being $\Theta(1)$ means that $\sigma$ is bounded above
and velow by a constant.

\subsection{Two Alternative Smoothness Indicators}
We propose two alternative ways of defining a smoothness indicator

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Simple smoothness indicator}~\cite{WENOAOSimplifiedSmoothness}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simpsmoothnessIC}
\sigma_{\text{simp}} = \frac{1}{N_\mathcal{S} -1 } \sum_k (\overline{u}_k - \overline{u}_0)^2,
\end{equation}
where $\overline{u}_0$ is the cell averaged solution in the target cell.
This simple smoothness indicator is designed to reduce the computational cost and to deal with
distorted stencils on unstructured meshes. However, this simple smoothness indicator is not as robust
as the classic Jiang-Shu smoothness indicator.

\item \textit{Polynomial smoothness indicator}~\cite{sigmaP}
		  \begin{equation}\label{eq:polysigma}
					 \sigma_{P} = \sum_{\alpha} \zeta_{\alpha, \alpha} c_{\alpha}^2
		  \end{equation}
		  which is obtained by dropping all the cross terms appeared in the formulation~\eqref{eq:cofformulation}.
		  This alternative serves as a good approximation to the classic Jiang-Shu smoothness indicator while the 
		  computing efficiency is greatly improved.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{ML-WENO weighting}
Given a target cell $E_{i,j}$, we can create a nonempty set of stencils $\{\mathcal{S}_l \ni E_{i,j}, l = 0,1, \dots, L, L \geq 0\}$.(See Figure~\ref{fig:quadmesh} for an example.)
The reconstruction of $u$ on the target cell $E_{i,j}$ is a convex combination of the stencil polynomials defined in equation~\eqref{eq:normalizedstencilpoly}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reconstruction}
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{l} \tilde{\omega_l} Q^{\mathcal{S}_l}(\mathbf{x}), \quad \mathbf{x} \in E_{i,j},
\end{equation}
where $\sum_l \tilde{\omega}_l = 1$ and $\tilde{\omega}_l$ are the normalized nonlinear weights associated with stencils.
\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\input chapters/tikz/mlstencil.tex
\caption{An example of a (3,2,1) multi-level stencils combination on a logically rectangular 
quadrilateral mesh.\label{fig:quadmesh}}
\end{figure}
We begin by arbitrarily choosing a set of linear weights $\{\omega_l\}$ and scale them with stencil smoothness indicator as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonlinearwgts}
\hat{\omega}_l = \frac{\omega_l}{(\sigma_l + \epsilon h_0^2)^{ar_l + n_l}},
\end{equation}
where $r_l = \max\{s,t\} = r$ in the cases of using square stencils, 
and the constants $\epsilon$ = 1e-4 and $a > 0.5$ are chosen accordingly. The biasing factor $n_l = n(r_l)$ 
is used to biasing further away from the constant level, whose smoothness indicator is always zero. We choose 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:biasingfctor}
n(r_l) = n_l = \begin{cases}
1, \quad &\text{if} \quad r_l = 1,\\
3, \quad &\text{if} \quad r_l = 2,\\
4, \quad &\text{if} \quad r_l \geq 3.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
We finally renormalise the scaled weights as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalizednonlinearwgts}
\tilde{\omega}_l = \frac{\hat{\omega}_l}{\sum_{l'} \hat{\omega}_{l'}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The ML-WENO Reconstruction Error}
In this subsection, we analyze the accuracy of the new multi-level weighting scheme.
Consider the set containing indices of stencils $l \in \mathcal{L} = \{0,1,2,\dots L\}$. 
%according to the order of the stencil polynomials.
Specifically, we denote the set of indices of
stencils of size $s\times t$ as $\mathcal{L}_{s,t} \subset \mathcal{L}$.
We assume the mesh resolution is sufficiently fine such that 
at least one smooth stencil exists in the presence of shocks. Accordingly, we define two index sets:
one containing the stencils identified as smooth, and the other containing those that exhibit discontinuities as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:groupstencil}
\begin{aligned}
		  \text{Smooth} &= \{l:\hspace{0.2cm} \text{$u$ is smooth on $\mathcal{S}_l$}\},\\
		  \text{Jump}   &= \{l:\hspace{0.2cm} \text{$u$ has a jump on $\mathcal{S}_l$}\},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Smooth} \cup \text{Jump} = \mathcal{L}$ and $\text{Smooth} \cap \text{Jump} = \O$. Moreover, we assume $\text{Smooth}\neq \O$
%We represent the set of indices of stencils of size $(s\times t)$ that is smooth as $\mathcal{L}_{s,t} \cap \text{Smooth}$,
%and the set of stencils of size $(s\times t)$ that has a jump as $\mathcal{L}_{s,t} \cap \text{Jump}$.
%For convenience, we assume square stencils, where $r=s=t$. 
%The accuracy result can be applied to $s\neq t$ situation directly in the selected direction.
We show that the reconstruction is accurate with respect to the target cell $E_0$ to order
\begin{equation}\label{eq:maxr}
r_{\max} = \max\{r: \text{Smooth} \cap \mathcal{L}_{r,r}  \neq \O \}.
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:stencilshock} illustrates a (3,2,1) combination of stencils in the presence of two shocks.
In this example, the set containing indices of smooth stencils is $\text{Smooth} = \{0, 1 \} $, indicating 
that $u$ on stencils $\mathcal{S}_0$ and $\mathcal{S}_1$ does not have a jump.
Consequently, we expect the accuracy of the reconstruction is $r_{\max} = \text{Smooth} \cap \mathcal{L}_{2,2} = 2$.

We follow the estimation of reconstruction error in~\cite{MultiLevelWeno}.
We start by estimating the error of the nonlinear scaling~\eqref{eq:nonlinearwgts} 
associated with some stencils $\mathcal{S}_l$
for some $m>0$.
\begin{equation}
\hat{\omega}_l = \frac{\omega_l}{(\sigma_l + \epsilon h_0^2)^m}.
\end{equation}
We estimate the reconstruction error under two distinct situtaions:
\begin{itemize}
\item $l \in \text{Smooth}$, the smoothness indicator $\sigma_l = Ch^2 + \mathcal{O}(h^3)$; 
\item $l \in \text{Jump}$, the smoothness indicator $\sigma_l = \Theta(1)$.
\end{itemize}
We employ the asymptotic estimation result from~\cite{olver1997asymptotics}. 
Assuming $n\geq m$, 
$a>0$, and $b>0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:asymptoticresult}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{a}{bh^m + \mathcal{O}(h^n)} &= \frac{ah^{-m}}{b} \big(1 + \mathcal{O}(h^{n-m})\big),\\
\frac{a}{bh^{-n} + \mathcal{O}(h^{-m})} &= \frac{ah^{n}}{b} \big(1 + \mathcal{O}(h^{n-m})\big).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For the smooth stencil $\mathcal{S}_l,\,  l\in \text{Smooth}$
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\omega}_l = \frac{\omega_l}{(\sigma_l+ \epsilon h_0^2)^m} &= \frac{\omega_l}{((C+\epsilon)h^2_0 + \mathcal{O}(h^3))^m}\\
               &= \omega_l \Big(\frac{h_0^{-2}}{C+\epsilon}(1+\mathcal{O}(h))  \Big)^m \\
					&= \frac{\omega_l}{(D+\epsilon)^m}h_0^{-2m} (1+\mathcal{O}(h)) .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In conclusion, we estimate the nonlinear scaling as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:estimatenonlinscale}
\hat{\omega}_l = \left\{  
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\omega_l}{(C+\epsilon)^m}h_0^{-2m} (1+\mathcal{O}(h)), \hspace{0.2cm} \text{if} \quad l \in \text{Smooth},\\
&\Theta (1), \hspace{3.83cm} \text{if} \quad l \in \text{Jump}.
\end{aligned}
      \right.
\end{equation}
Then we estimate the error for the normalized nonlinear weighting~\eqref{eq:normalizednonlinearwgts}.
We expand the sum of nonlinear scaling as
\begin{equation}
		  \sum_l \hat{\omega}_l = \sum_r \sum_{\substack{l\in \\\text{Smooth}\cap \mathcal{L}_{r,r}}} \hat{\omega}_l + \sum_{l\in \text{Jump}} \hat{\omega}_l.
\end{equation}
We estimate the smooth part of the nonlinear weighting as
\begin{equation}
\sum_r \sum_{\substack{l\in \\\text{Smooth}\cap \mathcal{L}_{r,r}}} \hat{\omega}_l = \sum_r \sum_{\substack{l\in \\\text{Smooth}\cap \mathcal{L}_{r,r}}} 
		  \frac{\omega_l}{(C+\epsilon)^m}h_0^{-2(ar+\eta)} (1+\mathcal{O}(h)).
\end{equation}
Assume $h<1$, we keep the largest $ar+\eta(r)$ number as our estimator
\begin{equation}
		  \begin{aligned}
					 \sum_r \sum_{\substack{l\in \\\text{Smooth}\cap \mathcal{L}_{r,r}}} \hat{\omega}_l = \sum_{\substack{l\in \\\text{Smooth}\cap \mathcal{L}_{r_{\max},r_{\max}}}} 
					 \frac{\omega_l}{(C+\epsilon)^{ar_{\max} + \eta(r_{\max})}}&h_0^{-2(ar_{\max}+\eta(r_{\max}))} (1+\mathcal{O}(h)) \\
					 &+ \Theta(h^{-2(ar+\eta(r))})\\
					 = \sum_{\substack{l\in \\\text{Smooth}\cap \mathcal{L}_{r_{\max},r_{\max}}}}
					 \frac{\omega_l}{(C+\epsilon)^{ar_{\max} + \eta(r_{\max})}}&h_0^{-2(ar_{\max}+\eta(r_{\max}))} \\
					 &+ \mathcal{O}(h^{1-2(ar_{\max}+\eta(r_{\max}))}).
		  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
later, we denote the set $\text{Smooth}\cap \mathcal{L}_{r,r}$ as $\text{Smooth}_r$ for 
simplicity.
The overall accuracy estimation of the nonlinear weighting~\eqref{eq:normalizednonlinearwgts} is presented as
\begin{itemize}
\item $l \in \text{Smooth}$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalliingestimate}
					 \tilde{\omega}_l = \frac{\hat{\omega}_l}{\sum_{l'} \hat{\omega}_{l'}} =
					 \frac{\omega_l}{\sum_{l' \in \text{Smooth}_r}\omega_{l'}} 
					 h^{2[a(r_{\max} - r) + \eta(r_{\max}) - \eta(r)]} (1 + \mathcal{O}(h));
\end{equation}

\item $l \in \text{Jump}$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalliingestimate}
					 \tilde{\omega}_l = \frac{\hat{\omega}_l}{\sum_{l'} \hat{\omega}_{l'}} =
					 \Theta
					 (h^{2[a(r_{\max}) + \eta(r_{\max})]}).
\end{equation}

\end{itemize}
Now we can estimate the reconstruction error as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\lvert u(\mathbf{x}) - \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}) \rvert &= \lvert \sum_{l}\tilde{\omega}_l (u(\mathbf{x} - Q_l(\mathbf{x})))  \rvert\\
&\leq \sum_{l \in \text{Smooth}} \tilde{\omega}_l \lvert u(\mathbf{x}) - Q_l(\mathbf{x}) \rvert + \sum_{l \in \text{Jump}} \tilde{\omega}_l \lvert u(\mathbf{x}) - Q_l(\mathbf{x}) \rvert \\
&\leq \sum_r \sum_{\substack{l\in \text{Smooth}_r}} \Theta(h^{2[a(r_{\max} - r) + (\eta(r_{\max}) - \eta(r))]}) \mathcal{O}(h^r) \\
&\quad \qquad + \sum_{l\in \text{Jump}} \Theta (h^{2(ar_{\max}+\eta(r_{\max}))}) \mathcal{O}(1) \\
&= \mathcal{O}(h^{r_{\max}})
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Combining everything above and~\eqref{eq:reconstruction}, we arrive at 
the formal error estimate of the reconstruction.
\begin{lem}
		  Let $\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x})$ be the reconstruction. Assuming a 
		  qusi-uniform mesh, $h_0 = \Theta(h)$, there exists a constant $C>0$
		  such that for any $\mathbf{x} \in E_0$, 
		  \begin{equation}\label{eq:errorestimate}
					 \lvert u(\mathbf{x}) - \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}) \rvert \leq C h^{r_{\max}},
		  \end{equation}
		  where $r_{\max}$ is defined by~\eqref{eq:maxr}.
\end{lem}

\subsection{Time stepping}
In this subsection, we briefly discuss some suitable time integration techniques. 
We focus on explicit schemes for two main reasons.
\begin{itemize}
\item The phase package cannot be accurately coupled over long time intervals;
\item The Jacobian is difficult to evaluate due to the involvement of an MFEM solver in velocity computation.
\end{itemize}
While forward Euler method is sufficient for stability analysis,
it only provides first-order accuracy. Therefore, we employ the strong-stability-
preservation (SSP) scheme introduced in~\cite{doi:10.1137/S003614450036757X}.
This scheme achieves higher order time accuracy while theoretically preserving 
the stability properties of the forward Euler method.

To begin with, the first-order forward Euler method is written as
\begin{equation}
u^{n+1} = u^n + \Delta t \tilde{\mathbf{F}}(u^n),
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{F}}(u^n)$ represents the numerical integration of the numerical flux $F(u^n)$
in Equation~\eqref{eq:fvdis}.
We then state a multi-stage explicit SSP Runge-Kutta  method as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multistageSSP}
\begin{aligned}
		  u^{0} &= u^n ,\\
		  u^{(i)}   &= \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} \alpha_{i,j}u^{(j)} + \Delta t \beta_{i,j} \tilde{\mathbf{F}}(u^{(j)}), \quad 1\leq i \leq m\\
		  u^{n+1} &= u^m,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\sum_{j}^{i-1} \alpha_{i,j} = 1$ for consistency. 
%In our computation, we have second order accuracy in MFEM solver, and a MLWENO(3,2)
%reconstruction in FVM. Hence the highest order of time stepping accurcay is three.
In consistent with the MFEM solver's second-order accuracy in velocity, we omit further discussion 
of time-integration schemes beyond third-order accuracy.
The second- and third-order explicit SSP Runge-Kutta schemes can be expressed in the 
multi-stage form~\eqref{eq:multistageSSP} as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
		  u^{0}   &= u^n\\
		  u^{(1)} &= u^{(0)} + \Delta t \tilde{\mathbf{F}}(u^{(0)})\\
		  u^{n+1} &= u^{(0)} + \frac{1}{2} \Delta t \tilde{\mathbf{F}}(u^{(0)}) + \frac{1}{2} \Delta t \tilde{\mathbf{F}}(u^{(1)}),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and 
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
		  u^{0}   &= u^n ,\\
		  u^{(1)} &= u^{(0)} + \Delta t \tilde{\mathbf{F}}(u^{(0)}),\\
		  u^{(2)} &= \frac{3}{4} u^n + \frac{1}{4} u^{(1)} + \frac{1}{4} \Delta t \tilde{\mathbf{F}}(u^{(1)})\\
		  u^{n+1} &= \frac{1}{3} u^{(0)}+\frac{2}{3}u^{(2)} + \frac{2}{3}\Delta t \tilde{\mathbf{F}}(u^{(2)}).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Handling Boundary Conditions}
We couple MFEM and FVM solver on a single mesh in our simulation. Therefore, 
accurate and compatible boundary conditions are assigned accordingly.
However, it is usually difficult to manage boundary conditions for higher order finite volume and finite difference method, which
require wide stencils. 

Some methods address this difficulty by using ghost cells or ghost points outside the computational domain, 
allowing the use of interior-like stencils near boundaries. However, the extrapolation or estimation of the 
manufactured values of ghost cells or ghost points are not straightforward.
A notable method, the inverse
Lax-Wendroff procedure, iteratively substitute normal derivatives
of the solution with tangential and time derivatives using the PDE relation to impose more precise values
for the ghost cells or ghost points.

We focus on utilizing the flexibility of the ML-WENO reconstruction to
tackle the difficulties in a direct way without the use 
of ghost cells. This approach is independent of the 
complexity of the governing partial differential equations and intricacies 
of the geometry of the boundary. Similar discussion using the adaptive 
order reconstruction can be found in~\cite{Semplice2020} and~\cite{Semplice2023}.

In Finite Volume Methods, we must use the boundary condition to determine
the numerical flux on the boundary.
To assign proper boundary conditions to a FVM discretization, 
it is necessary to first identify
whether the flow is inflow or outflow first.
Let $u_B$ denote the value of $u$ on the boundary. We classify the
types of boundary conditions based on the magnitude and direction of the numerical
flux $\mathbf{f}(u_B)\cdot \pmb{\nu}$.
\begin{itemize}
		  \item If $\mathbf{f}(u_B)\cdot \pmb{\nu} >0$, it is classified as an 
					 \textit{outflow}
boundary, and no fixed boundary condition should be assigned. Instead a 
\textit{free outflow} boundary condition is used, where the 
numerical flux is simply given by $\mathbf{f}(u_B) \cdot 
\pmb{\nu}$. The outflow boundary may lead to degradation in the accuracy of the 
					 numerical solution, as high-order stencils can extend beyond
					 the computational domain.
					 ML-WENO offers the flexibility to add stencils of the desired 
					 size that span back into the domain, thereby preserving
					 accuracy near outflow boundaries.

%The key to the above strategy is to have accurate and stable reconstruction near the boundary.
\item If $\mathbf{f}(u_B)\cdot \pmb{\nu} < 0$, it is classified as an 
		  \textit{inflow}
		  boundary. We can understand it as prescribing the inflow flux $\mathbf{f}_B$ 
or fixing the boundary
value $u_B$ as an equivalent to Dirichlet boundary in the context of Finite
Element Method. 
In the inflow case, discontinuous shock may enter the domain, 
requiring a swift response inside the domain to align with the specified
Dirichlet value. To accommodate such potential discontinuities, 
we can incorporate an extra constant stencil polynomial 
to the inflow boundary.

\item If $\mathbf{f}(u_B)\cdot \pmb{\nu} = 0$,  it is usually referred to
		  as a \textit{noflow} boundary. 
					 No additional treatment on the boundary is required, as
					 the value is fixed at zero.
\end{itemize}

In the following subsections, we present two examples: the first
example illustrates
how additional higher order stencils affect reconstruction order, 
while the second address an inflow Dirichlet
boundary condition that generates an incoming shock wave.

%boundary, e.g., when symmetry should be enforced.
