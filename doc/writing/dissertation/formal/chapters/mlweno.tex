\section{Finite Volume Method}
The finite volume method (FVM) is a discretization method widely used in
various scenarios that conservation laws are involved.
The finite volume is a robust algorithm that can be applied to 
arbitriary complex geometry. 
The most important aspect of FVM that benefits this simulation is
that the local mass conservation is achieved naturally.
To begin with, we consider a general conservation law 
retarding a scalar variable $u$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conservationlaw}
		  \left \{
		  \begin{aligned}
					 &\frac{\partial u(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{f}(u; \mathbf{x},t) = 0, \hspace{0.2cm} t>0, \mathbf{x} \in \Omega, \\ 
					 &u(\mathbf{x},0) = u_0(\mathbf{x}), \hspace{2.5cm} \mathbf{x} \in \Omega,
		  \end{aligned}
		  \right .
\end{equation}
where we assume the domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$. The term $\mathbf{f}$ is 
a flux transporting scalar variable $u$.
We write the basic semi-discretized form of basic finite volume method as
\begin{equation}
		  \frac{\partial \overline{u}}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{\lvert E \rvert}\int_{\partial E} \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},t) \cdot \pmb{\nu} d\mathbf{x} = 0,
\end{equation}
where we use the cell-averaged value $\overline{u} = \frac{1}{\lvert E \rvert} \int_{E} u(\mathbf{x}, t) d\mathbf{x}$, 
and $F(\mathbf{x},t)$ is the approximated numerical flux. 
We use the Lax-Friedrichs numerical flux
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LFflux}
		  \mathbf{F}(u^+, u^-) = \frac{1}{2}[(\mathbf{f}(u^+)+\mathbf{f}(u^-))\cdot \pmb{\nu} - \alpha_{LF}(u^+-u^-)],
\end{equation}
where $u^+$ and $u^-$ represent values approximated from element $E^+ \in \mathcal{T}_h$ and $E^- \in \mathcal{T}_h$ respectively,
and $\pmb{\nu}$ is the unit vector pointing from $E^+$ to $E^-$ orthogonal to the edge.
Here, $\alpha_{LF}$ is the Lax-Fredrich stabilizer factor. We have two different ways to compute $\alpha_{LF}$ depending on 
the knowledge of solution.
\begin{itemize}
		  \item The global factor: $\alpha_{LF} = \max_{u}\lvert \partial \mathbf{f}\cdot \pmb{\nu} / \partial u \rvert $;
		  \item The local factor: $\alpha_{LF} = \max(\lvert \partial \mathbf{f}^+ \cdot \pmb{\nu} / \partial u^+ \rvert,
					 \lvert \partial \mathbf{f}^- \cdot \pmb{\nu} / \partial u^- \rvert)$.
\end{itemize}
The numerical flux $\mathbf{F}$~\eqref{eq:LFflux} is consistent and conservtive across the edge. 
We now replace the $u^+$ and $u^-$ with reconstructed values and rewrite the numerical flux as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reconstructionflux}
		  \mathbf{F}(\mathcal{R}^+, \mathcal{R}-) = \frac{1}{2}[(\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{R}^+)+\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{R}^-)\cdot \pmb{\nu} - \alpha_{LF}(\mathcal{R}^+-\mathcal{R}^-)],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}$ is a higher order reconstruction value.

\subsection{Stencil Polynomial}
Given a stencil $\mathcal{S} = \{E_0, E_2, \dots, E_k\}$ of the quasiuniform logically rectangular mesh. We can define
an associated tensor product stencil polynomial 
$Q_{s,t} \in \mathbb{Q}_{(s,t)} \vcentcolon= \mathbb{P}_{s-1}(x) \otimes \mathbb{P}_{t-1}(y)$,
where $s$ and $t$ equal to the stencil sizes in the 
x- and y-directions, respectively.
We scale our stencil polynomial individually as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:stencilpolynomial}
		  Q_{s,t} = \sum_{p<s, q<t} c_{p,q} \Big(\frac{x - x_\mathcal{S}}{h_\mathcal{S}}\Big)^p 
		  \Big(\frac{y - y_\mathcal{S}}{h_\mathcal{S}}\Big)^q,
\end{equation}
where $(x_\mathcal{S},y_\mathcal{S})$ is the center point of the stencil 
$\Omega_\mathcal{S} = \cup_{E\in \mathcal{S}} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$, and $h_\mathcal{S} = 
\sqrt{\sum_{k}E_k/k}$ is the diameter of 
average cell size. Let $\xi = (x-x_\mathcal{S})/h_\mathcal{S}$ and $\eta = (y-y_\mathcal{S})/h_\mathcal{S}$ be the 
normalized varaibles, we can rewrite it into normalized version:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stencilpolynomialscaled}
		  \hat{Q}_{s,t} = \sum_{p<s, q<t} c_{p,q} \xi^p \eta^q.
\end{equation}

In finite volume scenerio, we are dealing with cell averaged value of the
scalar transport variable
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cellaveraged}
		  \overline{u}_E = \frac{1}{|E|}\int_E u(\mathbf{x}) d\mathbf{x} = \frac{1}{|E|} \int_{E}u(x,y) dx dy.
\end{equation}
We can calculate coefficents $c_{p,q}$ by imposing a constraint that cell averaged
value \eqref{eq:cellaveraged} is preserved through integral with stencil polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stencilpolycoefficent}
		  \frac{1}{|E_k|} \int_{E_k} Q(\mathbf{x}) d\mathbf{x} = \overline{u}_{E_k}, \quad 
		  \forall E_k \in \mathcal{S}.
\end{equation}
We will replace $\overline{u}_{E_k}$ with $\overline{u}_k$ in the following
discussion for simplicity.

We can represent stencil polynomial with basis polynomials 
$Q_{s,t} = \sum_{k} \overline{u}_{k} \phi^k_{s,t}$,
where basis polynomials can be calculated as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:basispoly}
		  \frac{1}{\lvert E_{k'}\rvert } \int_{E_{k'}} \phi^k_{s,t} (\mathbf{x}) d\mathbf{x} = 
		  \frac{1}{\lvert \hat{E}_{k'}\rvert} \iint_{\hat{E}_{k'}}\hat{\phi}^k_{s,t}(\xi, \eta) d\xi d\eta = \left \{
					 \begin{aligned}
								&1 \quad  k' = k\\
								&0 \quad  k' \neq k
					 \end{aligned}
					 \right. \quad \forall (E_{k'}, E_k) \in \mathcal{S},
\end{equation}
which would lead to a $(s \times t) \times (s \times t)$ non-singular linear system. 
We can now represent stencil polynomial with normalized basis polynomials as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalizedstencilpoly}
\hat{Q}_{s,t}(\xi, \eta) = \sum_k \overline{u}_k \hat{\phi}_{s,t}^k(\xi, \eta).
\end{equation}

We define a linear projection operator $\pi_\mathcal{S}$ and let $\pi_\mathcal{S} u_\mathcal{S} = Q$ be a linear 
projection onto polynomial space $W_p$ such that $\mathbb{Q}_{s,t} \subset W_p$.
We estimate interpolation error with the Bramble-Hilbert Lemma~\cite{PolynomialApproximation} 
and~\cite{EstimationofLinearFunctionals} in $H_r(\Omega)$ space, where $r = s$ in x direction and

\subsection{Stencil polynomial smoothness indicators}
We define a smoothness indicator $\sigma$ as a measurement of 
smoothness of $u(\mathbf{x})$ on the stencil $S$. 
The classic method from~\cite{JSSmoothness} is defined by Sobolev seminorm as:
\begin{equation}
		  \sigma_P = \sum_{1\leq \lvert\alpha\rvert \leq m} 
		  \int\int_{E_0} \lvert E_0 \rvert^{\lvert\alpha\rvert -1} 
		  (\mathcal{D}^\alpha Q(x,y))^2 dxdy,
\end{equation}
where $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ is a multi-index, $\lvert \alpha \rvert = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2$, 
and $\mathcal{D}$ is the derivative operator $\mathcal{D}^\alpha = \partial^{\lvert \alpha \rvert} / \partial x^{\alpha_1} \partial y^{\alpha_2}$.
The Jiang-Shu smoothness indicator can be reformed with basis polynomials:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:JSsmoothenssIC}
\begin{aligned}
		  \sigma_P &= \sum_{1\leq \lvert \alpha \rvert \leq m} \lvert E_0 \rvert^{\lvert \alpha \rvert -1} \iint_{E_0} (\mathcal{D}^\alpha Q(x,y))^2 dx dy\\
					  &= \sum_{1\leq \lvert \alpha \rvert \leq m} \iint_{\hat{E}_0} (\mathcal{D}^\alpha\hat{Q}(\xi, \eta))^2 d\xi d\eta \\
					  &= \sum_{1\leq \lvert \alpha \rvert \leq m} \iint_{\hat{E}_0} \big( \sum_{ij} \overline{u}_{ij} \mathcal{D}^\alpha \hat{\phi}_{i,j}(\xi, \eta) \big)^2 d\xi d\eta \\
					  &= \sum_{ij} \sum_{i'j'} \overline{u}_{ij} \overline{u}_{i'.j'} \sum_{1\leq \lvert \alpha \rvert \leq m} 
					  \iint_{\hat{E}_{ij}} \mathcal{D}^\alpha \hat{\phi}_{ij}(\xi, \eta) 
					  \mathcal{D}^\alpha \hat{\phi}_{i'j'}(\xi, \eta) d\xi d\eta \\
					  &= \sum_{ij} \sum_{i'j'} \overline{u}_{ij} \overline{u}_{i'j'} \sigma^{i'j'}_{ij},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma^{i'j'}_{ij}$ can be precomputed.

We proposed an alternative way of defining smoothness indicator in~\cite{WENOAOSimplifiedSmoothness}.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simpsmoothnessIC}
\sigma_{\text{simp}} = \frac{1}{N_\mathcal{S} -1 } \sum_k (\overline{u}_k - \overline{u}_0)^2,
\end{equation}
where $\overline{u}_0$ is the cell averaged solution in target cell. 
We define this simple smoothness indicator in order to reduce the compuational cost and to deal with
distorted stencil on unstructured mesh. However, this simple smoothness indicator is not as robust
as the classic Jiang-Shu smoothness indicator. Hence, we stick to the classic definition in the following
discussion.

We observe that the smoothness indicator exhibit following convergence property that
there exists some value $C > 0$ such that when $h \rightarrow 0^+$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smoothconvergence}
		  \sigma = \begin{cases}
					 Ch^2 + \mathcal{O} (h^3) \quad &\text{Smooth}, \\
					 \Theta (h)  &\text{Not Smooth},
		  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where stencils are defined on quasi uniform mesh. This observation comes directly from the scaling property of 
Sobolev seminorm.

\subsection{ML-WENO weighting}
Given a target cell $E_{i,j}$, we can create a nonempty set of stencils $\mathcal{S}_l \ni E_{i,j}, l = 0,1, \dots, L$.
The reconstruction of u on target cell $E_{i,j}$ is a convex combination of stencil polynomials~\eqref{eq:normalizedstencilpoly}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reconstruction}
R(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{l} \tilde{\omega_l} Q^l(\mathbf{x}), \quad \mathbf{x} \in E_{i,j},
\end{equation}
where $\sum_l \tilde{\omega}_l = 1$ is normalized nonlinear weights associated with stencils accordingly.

We begin by arbitrarily chosing a set of linear weights $\{\omega_l\}$ and scale with stencil smoothness indicator as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonlinearwgts}
\hat{\omega}_l = \frac{\omega_l}{(\sigma_l + \epsilon h_0^2)^{ar_l + n_l}},
\end{equation}
where $r_l = \max\{s,t\}$, $\epsilon = 1e-4$ and $a > 0.5$ are picked as constants. The biasing factor $n_l = n(r_l)$ 
is used to biasing away further from constant level, whose smoothness indicator is always zero, when the solution is 
smooth. We pick 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:biasingfctor}
n(r_l) = n_l = \begin{cases}
1, \quad &\text{if} \quad r_l = 1,\\
3, \quad &\text{if} \quad r_l = 2,\\
4, \quad &\text{if} \quad r_l \geq 3.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
We then renormalize scaled weights
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalizednonlinearwgts}
\tilde{\omega}_l = \frac{\hat{\omega}_l}{\sum_{l'} \hat{\omega}_{l'}}.
\end{equation}
